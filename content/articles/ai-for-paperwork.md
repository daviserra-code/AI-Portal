Paperwork is one of the most practical things to do with a [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot). You can paste in a rental contract, a letter from the tax office or a confusing form and ask what it means in everyday words. Plenty of people already do: in a UK survey by the legal charity [JUSTICE](https://justice.org.uk/reports/what-ai-chatbots-can-teach-us-about-unmet-legal-needs), about one in six people who had a legal dispute in the past two years had used an AI chatbot for help, and one participant pasted in a lawyer's email and asked for it in "normal person language".

That is the right way to think about it: a patient translator from official language into plain language. It is not a lawyer. The tools behind ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot are [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model), programs that predict likely wording. They are very good at rephrasing, and they can be wrong about the law, or about what your own document says, in a voice that sounds completely sure.

## What is AI actually good at with paperwork?

Used carefully, a chatbot saves real time on four jobs:

- **Summarising** a long contract or agreement so you know what you are signing.
- **Explaining** an official letter: who sent it, what they want, and by when.
- **Helping with forms**: what a question is asking, and which documents you will need.
- **Translating** a letter or reply so you can follow it.

In each case the AI gives you a first reading. You still check it against the paper in front of you. The prompts below are written to make that check easy: they ask the AI to quote the document, not just describe it. Replace the words in capitals with your own details.

## How do I get a contract or tenancy agreement summarised?

Most chatbots let you upload a file or a photo. OpenAI's [file uploads help page](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8555545-file-uploads-faq) says ChatGPT reads PDFs that contain text, and images, but not PDFs that are only a scan with no text in them. If a scanned PDF fails, a clear photo of each page usually works. Copying and pasting the text is fine too.

Then ask for a structured summary, with quotes:

> I am a tenant in COUNTRY. Summarise this tenancy agreement in plain English. List: the rent and when it is due, the deposit and how I get it back, how long the contract lasts, how either side can end it and with how much notice, what I am responsible for repairing, and any fees or penalties. For each point, quote the exact clause and give its number.

The quotes are the point. They let you find each claim in the original in seconds, and if the AI cannot quote a clause, that is a warning sign that it made the point up.

Two more prompts help before you sign:

> Which clauses in this contract are unusual or one-sided compared with a typical agreement of this kind? Quote each one and explain why it might matter to me.

> Write me a list of questions to ask the landlord, or an advice service, before I sign.

The second prompt is often the most useful thing a chatbot can produce, because it turns your vague worry into specific questions a human can answer. If the document is long, ask about one section at a time. Our guide to [writing a good prompt](/use/how-to-write-a-good-prompt) covers more ways to give context.

## How do I get an official letter explained?

Letters from tax offices, courts, benefits agencies, banks and debt collectors are written to be precise, not readable. A chatbot can untangle them:

> Explain this letter in simple words. Who sent it? What are they asking me to do? Is there a deadline, and what happens if I miss it? Quote the sentences that contain any date, amount or reference number.

Then check the date and amount yourself, on the letter, before you write them in your diary. A deadline is the one thing you cannot afford the AI to get wrong.

If you are not sure a letter is genuine, a chatbot can point out common warning signs, but it cannot tell you for certain. Contact the sender using a phone number or address you find yourself, not one printed on the letter. Our guide to [AI for older people](/live-with-it/ai-for-older-people) has more on scams.

You can also ask for help with a reply:

> Draft a short, polite reply asking for more time to pay, explaining that I am waiting for my salary on DATE. Do not admit anything or promise anything beyond that.

Read the draft as if you were the person receiving it. You will be held to what it says, not to what you meant.

## Can AI help me fill in a form?

Yes, as a guide sitting next to you, not as the person signing it. Good questions to ask:

> This is a form for APPLYING FOR WHAT in COUNTRY. Explain what question 7 is asking, in plain words, and what kind of answer usually goes there.

> Make me a checklist of the documents I will probably need to complete this form.

Type your real answers into the official form yourself. The AI does not know your situation, and some forms, especially sworn statements, carry penalties for wrong answers. The [Tenants' Union of New South Wales](https://www.tenants.org.au/blog/ai-and-tenancy-advice-helpful-tool-or-hidden-risk) in Australia advises people not to use AI to write evidence, witness statements or statutory declarations at all.

Some newer [AI agents](/understand/ai-agents-explained) can fill in web forms for you. For anything official, don't let them press submit.

## What about paperwork in another language?

A chatbot can translate a letter well enough for you to understand it, and our guide to [emails, summaries and translations](/use/ai-for-everyday-writing) covers how good these translations are and a back-translation check. A useful prompt for paperwork:

> Translate this letter into English. Keep every date, amount and reference number exactly as written, and mark any sentence you are unsure about.

When you need to hand a translation to an authority, an AI translation is usually not enough. For documents such as birth, marriage or residence certificates moving between EU countries, the [Your Europe](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/family/couple/getting-public-documents-accepted/) portal says you can ask the issuing country for a multilingual standard form to present with the document "instead of presenting a translation", and that the receiving authority "may exceptionally ask you to provide a certified translation". Ask the office what it accepts before you start.

## Where does AI go wrong with legal paperwork?

The best-known study is from Stanford University. In January 2024, researchers [reported](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/hallucinating-law-legal-mistakes-large-language-models-are-pervasive) that the AI models they tested gave false answers to specific questions about US court cases between 69% and 88% of the time. These were older models, and newer ones are better, but the researchers' finding about false premises still matters: when a question assumed something untrue, a model often went along with it. They concluded that "the users who would benefit the most from legal LLM are precisely those who the LLMs are least well-equipped to serve."

Consumer tests point the same way. The UK consumer group [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/policy-and-insight/article/chatgpt-and-gemini-among-ai-tools-giving-risky-consumer-advice-which-finds-aBnBP0l2CE0T) put 40 consumer questions to six AI tools in September 2025. Asked about flight refunds, Copilot said you are always entitled to a full refund, which is not the case. Gemini advised withholding money from a builder if a job went wrong, which Which? warns can lead to a breach of contract. When a question contained a deliberate mistake about a savings limit, ChatGPT and Copilot did not correct it.

The Tenants' Union of New South Wales tested ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot in March 2026 on a tenant whose landlord wanted to move back in. Gemini invented a legal requirement its own sources did not mention, and two tools gave the impression a notice period was always 90 days when it can be 60.

From these tests, the common failures are:

- **Made-up content.** An invented clause, rule or court case. This is called a [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination), and our guide on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) explains why it happens.
- **The wrong country.** Tenancy, employment and consumer law differ between countries and even regions. Always say where you live.
- **Missing context.** The AI only knows the pages you showed it. A letter that refers to an earlier decision, or a contract with an annex you did not upload, will be explained without the missing part.
- **Going along with you.** If you ask "how do I get my full deposit back?", it may not tell you that you might not be entitled to it.

The stakes are real. A database of US court cases where AI-generated errors turned up in filings listed 1,058 cases by the start of June 2026, according to the [UNC Law Library](https://library.law.unc.edu/2026/06/handling-hallucinations-a-reflection/), and about 60% involved people representing themselves rather than lawyers.

## What should I never upload?

Before you upload a photo or a file, cover or delete what the AI does not need. It almost never needs:

- **ID numbers:** passport, national ID, tax or social security numbers.
- **Bank details:** account numbers, IBANs, card numbers, PINs and online banking codes.
- **Health details:** diagnoses, medical letters, prescriptions. Under EU data protection law, health-related data is one of the "sensitive" categories that get extra protection, according to the [European Commission](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/rules-business-and-organisations/legal-grounds-processing-data/sensitive-data/what-personal-data-considered-sensitive_en).
- **Other people's details:** a flatmate's name and income, a relative's medical letter.

Replace names with "Tenant" and "Landlord", and an account number with "XXXX". The explanation will be just as good. A photo can show more than you think, so crop it to the page and check corners and the back of the letter.

What you upload is usually stored, and on many personal accounts it may be used to train future AI systems unless you switch that off. OpenAI's help page says uploaded files are kept for as long as the chat they belong to. Our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) shows where the privacy settings are for each major chatbot. For medical paperwork, our piece on [AI and health questions](/live-with-it/ai-and-health-questions) goes further.

## When should I see a lawyer or an advice service?

Use AI to understand. Talk to a person before you act, especially before you:

- sign a contract, or agree to a change in one,
- pay money you think you might not owe,
- reply to a court, a tax office or a debt collector,
- miss a deadline, or decide one does not apply to you,
- withhold rent or payment in a dispute.

Help does not always cost money. Tenants' associations, consumer organisations, trade unions, legal aid offices and citizens' advice services often help for free. In the EU, [Your Europe Advice](https://europa.eu/youreurope/advice/) is a free service that answers questions about your EU rights, in any of the EU's 24 official languages, usually "within one week", though it does not give in-depth legal advice or take on cases. For a dispute with a business in another EU country, Norway or Iceland, the [European Consumer Centres Network](https://www.eccnet.eu/node/31) offers "free expert, confidential advice on consumer rights".

A chatbot is still useful here. Ask it to prepare you: a timeline of what happened, a list of your documents, and the questions you want answered. You will get more out of a short appointment, and the person across the desk can check what the AI told you.
