This was the week the fight over what chatbots say became part of election campaigning. With the US midterms on 3 November, reporters found people trying to tilt the answers voters get. Here are the seven stories that matter most.

## Campaigns are trying to shape what chatbots tell voters

More voters now ask [chatbots](/glossary/chatbot) about candidates, and campaigns have noticed. Documents reviewed by Reuters, [as summarised by International Business Times](https://www.yahoo.com/news/politics/articles/ai-users-asking-chatbots-deserves-173635008.html) on 26 October, include a pitch for a Republican candidate for governor of Massachusetts proposing hundreds of policy pages written for AI to pick up, plus posts on YouTube and Reddit. The report does not show the plan was used or that it worked. On the Democratic side, a tool called CampSight checks what chatbots say about progressive candidates, and its makers say "hundreds of campaigns" use it.

A sharper case came from [NPR on 30 October](https://www.ijpr.org/npr-news/2026-10-30/websites-pretending-to-be-local-news-are-boosting-democratic-candidates). It found a network of 37 websites, many dressed up as local news, that promote Democratic House candidates. All were set up on 15 September, and NPR could not find out who runs them. The Democratic campaign committee denied involvement. In NPR's tests, ChatGPT cited the network in about half of 37 answers about competitive races. Google's Gemini cited none of them.

What it means: a chatbot is only as good as the pages it reads, and those pages can be planted. The weakest spots are small races with little real coverage. For voting details, go to your official election office. For candidates, use sources you recognise. Our guide to [AI and elections](/live-with-it/ai-and-elections) has a short routine for checking what you see.

## New York bans chatbots from playing friend to children

On 28 October New York's governor Kathy Hochul [signed a law](https://www.governor.ny.gov/news/governor-hochul-signs-nation-leading-legislation-protect-kids-ai-chatbots-and-ensure-baby) on chatbots and under-18s. Chatbots may not pose as a real person, claim human feelings, or claim a romantic or personal relationship with a child. They may not encourage self-harm, eating disorders or drug use, or urge children to keep secrets from parents. The rules also apply to users whose age a company has not confirmed.

Companies must check ages in more than one way, including at least one that does not rely only on a government ID, and must delete the age data afterwards. The state attorney general can seek up to $25,000 per violation. [Fortune notes](https://fortune.com/2026/10/28/new-york-state-kathy-hochul-bans-ai-chatbots-romantic-baby-monitors) that Hochul also signed a second law requiring basic security on internet-connected baby monitors.

What it means: if you live in New York, expect more age checks in chatbot and companion apps. The governor's office says the chatbot rules take effect "in the coming months", without a date. Elsewhere, nothing changes yet. Our guide to [kids, teens and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/kids-teens-and-ai-chatbots) covers what to look for in the meantime.

## Anthropic's stock market filing spells out the risks it sees

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude chatbot, filed on 26 October to sell shares to the public, [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/anthropics-ipo-filing-shows-off-soaring-revenues-as-the-company-warns-of-ais-risks-to-huma). The filing shows $16.3 billion in revenue for the first half of 2026 and a net loss of $80.7 billion. [The New York Times explains](https://www.khaleejtimes.com/business/seven-numbers-that-explain-anthropics-business-after-ipo-announcement) that $72.4 billion of that loss is a one-off accounting charge linked to how some of its funding was structured.

The warnings drew more attention. According to [Euronews, citing AP](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/27/rogue-ai-cyberattacks-and-weapons-the-risks-anthropic-is-warning-investors-about-ahead-of-), the filing says AI systems could "take harmful actions independently and at scale without human intervention", that progress could move "beyond our ability to evaluate and safeguard new models", and that advanced systems could "pose catastrophic or existential risks to humanity" if misused or left to act with a lot of independence.

What it means: companies must list every serious risk to investors, so this is partly legal caution. Still, it is a frontier AI company putting these risks in writing, under legal liability. Our explainer on [AI safety](/super-intelligence/what-is-ai-safety) covers what these risks mean and who is working on them.

## Ads arrive inside Google's AI answers, and Reddit wants you logged in

Google said on 28 October that it is bringing ads to more people and searches in its AI Overviews and AI Mode, the AI-written answers at the top of results, and taking AI Mode ads beyond the US, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/28/google-expands-ai-search-ads-globally-as-core-ad-growth-slows/). TechCrunch calls it Google's first global push to make money from these features. It did not say which countries come first.

The next day Reddit said it will move towards requiring people to log in, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/29/as-search-traffic-dries-up-reddit-announces-its-shifting-to-a-logged-in-experience/). Its visits from search fell by more than a million a week on average in October, which TechCrunch puts down to AI summaries that answer questions without sending people on. Reddit says logging in also protects its posts from AI companies collecting them without a deal. Tests start outside the US this quarter.

What it means: an AI answer can now contain paid placements, so look for the sponsored label before trusting a product tip. And more of the free web may sit behind logins as sites try to stop AI from taking their content.

## Norway's newsrooms take Meta to court over AI terms

Ten Norwegian media groups, including the public broadcaster NRK, asked an Oslo court on 27 October to block new Meta terms for business accounts, [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/27/norwegian-media-sue-meta-over-plans-to-use-their-journalism-for-ai). The terms would let Meta collect articles, photos and videos posted on Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp and Threads, including paywalled work, and use them to build AI products. The only way out is to delete the account. After pushback, Meta delayed the change from 30 October to 20 November.

What it means: this affects any business with a page on Meta's apps, not just newspapers. It is part of a wider fight over whose work becomes AI [training data](/glossary/training-data). Our piece on [who owns AI-generated content](/live-with-it/who-owns-ai-generated-content) explains the background.

## One in three young adults ask chatbots about drugs and alcohol

A study in JMIR Formative Research of 995 US adults aged 18 to 29 found that 32% had used chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini or Meta AI for information on drugs or alcohol, [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/26/one-in-three-young-adults-use-ai-chatbots-for-drug-and-alcohol-information-study-finds). Most asked about risks, and about half asked about specific substances, including doses. The lead author, Mireia Triguero Roura of Rutgers University, warned that this "can lead to misinformed or otherwise risky substance-use behaviour".

What it means: chatbots can sound sure and still be wrong, a problem called [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination). On doses and mixing substances, a wrong answer can hurt you. Check with a pharmacist, a doctor or an official health service. Our guide on [AI and health questions](/live-with-it/ai-and-health-questions) explains how to use a chatbot safely here.

## Australia says AI drove a jump in cyberattacks

Australia's cyber security agency, the Australian Signals Directorate, said in its yearly threat report on 27 October that AI "has been the defining force in the cyber threat landscape over the past year", [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/27/australia-warns-of-unprecedented-ai-powered-cyberattacks-heres-whats-driving-them). It says AI helps criminals and state hackers find and exploit weak spots faster, and it sent more than 4,400 warnings about suspicious activity, up 80% on the year before.

What it means: this follows [last week's roundup](/whats-new/week-in-ai-2026-10-25), when AI-assisted bug hunting took sites across Europe offline. The basic advice still works: install updates on your phone and computer promptly, and turn on two-step login for important accounts.
