Ask a [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) for "four relaxed days in Lisbon with a toddler, no museums before 10" and you get a sensible first draft in seconds. That is the part AI is genuinely good at. The trouble starts when the draft contains a restaurant, a trail or a visa rule that isn't real, and nothing in the answer tells you which bits those are.

So the useful way to think about AI travel planning is this: let it do the brainstorming and the legwork, then check every fact that could cost you money or leave you stuck.

## What is AI actually good at when planning a trip?

Chatbots run on [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model), which are very good at shaping messy wishes into a tidy plan. In practice, that makes them useful for:

- **First-draft itineraries.** Give it dates, budget, pace and who is coming, and it will sort sights into days that make rough sense. You then fix what doesn't fit.
- **Comparing options.** "Train or fly from Ljubljana to Vienna, what are the trade-offs?" is a question chatbots answer well in general terms: time, hassle, luggage, typical price ranges.
- **Packing lists.** Tell it the climate, the activities and the length of the trip, and you get a checklist you can edit. Low stakes, high usefulness.
- **Translation.** Chatbots can translate a menu, a sign or a polite complaint to a landlord. Google says its Translate app can now play live spoken translations through any pair of headphones in more than 70 languages; in December 2025 that was a beta on Android in the US, Mexico and India, with iOS and more countries planned for 2026.
- **A first look at entry rules.** A chatbot can tell you which kinds of documents usually matter for a trip, so you know what to look up. It should be where your research starts, not where it ends (more on that below).

The better your request, the better the plan. Our guide to [writing a good prompt](/use/how-to-write-a-good-prompt) explains how to give context, and [comparing the main chatbots](/use/chatbots-compared) shows which ones can search the web for current information.

## Which AI travel tools can you use now?

Beyond general chatbots, the big search and booking companies are building AI straight into travel.

**Google.** In November 2025, according to TechRadar, Google added trip-planning tools to AI Mode in Search, including itineraries that pull in live data from Google Flights, Hotels and Maps. On 27 August 2026, TechCrunch reported, Google added flight price tracking in AI Mode (in more than 180 countries) and hotel booking through the conversation, paid with Google Pay. The hotel booking started in the US, in English, with partners including Booking.com, Expedia, Hilton, Marriott and others. Google says the hotel or booking site still handles the booking and any customer service.

**ChatGPT with Expedia and Booking.com.** Since October 2025, Expedia and Booking.com have offered apps inside ChatGPT that show hotels and, for Expedia, flights with live prices. At launch they were available only to users outside the EU, according to Expedia and PhocusWire, and the actual booking happened on the travel company's own site. OpenAI's broader [AI agent](/glossary/ai-agent), which can browse sites and fill in forms for you, is covered in our [AI agents explainer](/understand/ai-agents-explained).

**Airbnb.** TechCrunch reported on 1 October 2026 that Airbnb had just rolled out AI-powered search, with voice agents planned for later in the autumn.

These features change quickly and vary by country. If one isn't available where you live, that is normal.

## Where does AI travel advice go wrong?

The core problem is called [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination): a chatbot produces something that sounds right but isn't, with the same confident tone it uses for true facts. Our explainer on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) covers why it happens. In travel, it has already caused real trouble.

**Places that don't exist.** The BBC reported in September 2025 on two tourists in Peru planning an unguided hike to the "Sacred Canyon of Humantay", a place that does not exist. They were stopped by Miguel Angel Gongora Meza, a local tour operator, who warned that this kind of advice could leave someone "at an altitude of 4,000m without oxygen and [phone] signal," as Futurism quotes him.

**Wrong times.** The same BBC report described a couple who climbed Mount Misen in Japan after ChatGPT told them the last ropeway down ran until 5:30 pm. It had already closed when they reached the top, and they were stuck there.

**Wrong distances.** A traveller described on TikTok how an AI tool put a day trip to Ba Na Hills into her Vietnam itinerary from Hanoi, according to a Stacker roundup of cases. The trip is about 1,400 kilometres there and back.

**Wrong entry rules.** In March 2025, the Australian marketing strategist Mark Pollard missed a conference in Chile after ChatGPT wrongly told him he didn't need a visa, according to the OECD's AI incidents monitor, which collects news reports of such cases.

**AI-written travel content.** It isn't only chatbots. In early 2026, a small Australian tour company's website published an AI-generated blog post recommending "Weldborough Hot Springs" in Tasmania. There are no hot springs in Weldborough. The owner of the local hotel said she was getting about five calls a day and two or three visitors a day looking for them, The New Daily reports. The tour company's owner told the ABC: "our AI has messed up completely."

**Scams made with AI.** Booking.com's internet safety chief, Marnie Wilking, said in 2024 that travel scams had risen by 500 to 900 percent in about a year and a half, and linked part of that to tools like ChatGPT, Silicon UK reports. Scammers use AI to write convincing emails in many languages and to create fake images, including for fake hotels that disappear after taking payment. Our guide to [spotting AI-generated content](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content) can help with suspicious listings and photos.

## What should you always check with an official source?

A simple rule: if being wrong would stop you boarding, cost you a night's accommodation or put you somewhere unsafe, confirm it yourself.

**Entry rules, every time.** Rules change, and a chatbot may have learned an old version. Two current examples for travellers to Europe and the US:

- **EES (EU Entry/Exit System).** According to the European Commission, the EES has been fully running since 10 April 2026. Non-EU travellers on short stays register their name, passport details, fingerprints and a face photo at the border of the 29 European countries that use it. You don't apply in advance.
- **ETIAS (EU travel authorisation).** This is the online permit that visitors from countries that don't need a Schengen visa will need before visiting the Schengen area and Cyprus. The Commission said in April 2026 that it is scheduled to start in the last quarter of 2026. As of 19 October 2026, SchengenVisaInfo reported that no start date had been announced, and that once it does start there will be a six-month period in which it is optional. EU citizens don't need it.
- **ESTA (US).** Travellers from Visa Waiver Program countries need an ESTA before flying to the US. A US Customs and Border Protection notice on fees for the 2027 financial year puts it at about 40 US dollars per application.

If a chatbot tells you any of these rules confidently, check the date and the official page. The Commission's own advice is to apply for ETIAS only through the official website or app, to avoid "fraudulent third-party sites that may charge extra fees or misuse personal data." The same advice makes sense for ESTA.

**Also check yourself:**

- Opening hours, last entry and seasonal closures, on the venue's own website.
- Last trains, buses, ferries and cable cars of the day.
- Distances and travel times, in a map app rather than in the chatbot's text.
- That a hotel, restaurant or trail actually exists: recent reviews, photos from different people, an official address.
- Safety advice from your own foreign ministry for the country you are visiting.

## Is it safe to give an AI agent my passport and card details?

Be careful. An agent that books for you may need your name, date of birth, passport number and payment card. Every chat is stored for a while by the company, as our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) explains.

There is a newer risk too. Agents read web pages to do their job, and a page can hide instructions meant to hijack them, a trick called prompt injection. The UK's National Cyber Security Centre says current language models "simply do not enforce a security boundary" between your instructions and the text they read, and that "it's very possible that prompt injection attacks may never be totally mitigated."

Sensible habits:

- Let the AI research and compare, then book on the airline, hotel or booking site yourself.
- If you do let an agent book, keep its "ask before paying" confirmation switched on and watch what it does.
- Use a card with a low limit or a virtual card number if your bank offers one.
- Type passport details directly into the official form, not into the chat.
- Never type a [prompt](/glossary/prompt) that contains your full passport scan, PINs or security codes.

## What does a quick checklist look like?

Before you rely on an AI-made plan:

1. Ask the chatbot to list its sources, and open them. If it can't give any, treat the plan as a draft.
2. Check visa, ETIAS, ESTA or other entry rules on the official government site, and apply only there.
3. Confirm opening hours, last departures and seasonal closures on the operator's own website.
4. Look up every place in a map app, and check distances and travel times.
5. Make sure hotels and restaurants exist, with recent reviews from several sources.
6. Book and pay on the official site, and be wary of messages asking you to pay again or "verify" your card.
7. Keep a paper or offline copy of bookings and emergency numbers, in case your phone or the app fails.
