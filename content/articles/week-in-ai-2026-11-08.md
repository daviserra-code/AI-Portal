AI was on the ballot this week, though not in the way most people expected. In the US midterms of 3 November the issue that moved voters was not chatbots but the giant buildings that run them. Here are the six stories that matter most.

## Voters turned against AI data centres in the midterms

Data centres are the warehouse-sized buildings full of computers that train and run AI, and they use a lot of power and water. On 3 November voters in around 40 places, most of them in Ohio and Illinois, voted on local measures restricting them. All but one went the restrictive way, [The Intercept reports](https://theintercept.com/2026/11/05/midterm-elections-ai-data-centers-voters/), citing a tracker kept by Redbud Consulting. In an NBC News poll of voters on Election Day, about three in four opposed a data centre in their own community, and more than half said AI and data centres were a major factor in their vote.

Several Democrats who won governor and Senate races had promised to pause or restrict new data centres, including in Georgia, Ohio and Texas, according to The Intercept. Money from the AI industry did little to stop them. Super PACs on both sides of the AI debate spent $79 million across 68 House and Senate races, and most of the Republicans they backed in close races lost, [The Next Web reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/ai-super-pacs-midterms-results-leading-the-future). The one big win for industry money came earlier: in New York in June, a pro-industry group spent about $8.1 million against Alex Bores, a critic of the AI industry, in a congressional primary, and he lost. [Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/ai-politics/data-centers-were-the-big-loser-of-the-midterms-12665187) points to a Gallup survey from May in which seven in ten Americans opposed an AI data centre near them.

What it means: the cost of AI is becoming a local, practical question about electricity bills, water and land, not just a debate about chatbots. If you live outside the US, expect the same arguments wherever a big data centre is planned near you. Our explainer on [AI's energy and water use](/live-with-it/ai-energy-and-water) sets out what these buildings consume.

## AI shares had their worst day since 2020

On 5 November, two days after the vote, the Nasdaq 100 index of large US tech companies fell more than 6%, its worst day since March 2020, [Bloomberg reports](https://www.staradvertiser.com/2026/11/05/breaking-news/nasdaq-heads-for-correction-as-ai-stocks-plunge-after-midterms/). An index of chipmakers fell 12%, and Nvidia, the best-known maker of AI chips, lost more than 9%. Bloomberg says investors feared the new Congress could slow AI growth and the heavy spending on data centres.

[Reuters, via The Korea Times](https://www.koreatimes.co.kr/world/20261106/gathering-ai-storm-clouds-doom-tech-stocks-to-darkest-day-in-a-year), adds wider worries: doubts that the huge sums spent on AI will pay off, and local opposition to data centres. It quotes the bank UBS estimating that about 30% of the US data centre capacity expected in 2028 is at risk from construction pauses. "The market's moving from the promise of AI to the price of AI," one fund manager told Reuters.

What it means: many pension funds and index savings plans hold large amounts of these companies, so a fall like this reaches ordinary savers. One bad day says little about the long run, and this is not advice to buy or sell. It is a sign that investors are now asking whether AI will earn back what is being spent on it.

## A Brazilian court tells Meta to keep its AI away from the news

On 3 November a court in São Paulo ordered Meta to stop collecting content from Brazilian media to train its AI, [AFP reports](https://malaymail.com/news/tech-gadgets/2026/11/04/brazilian-media-win-first-round-against-meta-over-ai-training-demand/241900). Three national press associations had challenged new terms for business accounts on Facebook, Instagram and WhatsApp, which they say demand free access to their work. The judge suspended that part of the terms, barred Meta from punishing outlets that refuse them and set a fine of one million reais a day, about 180,000 US dollars, if Meta does not comply. He called the demand "a manifest abuse of rights". Meta did not immediately respond to AFP.

This follows the Norwegian case in [last week's roundup](/whats-new/week-in-ai-2026-11-01). [AP reports](https://jamaica-gleaner.com/article/business/20261103/meta-delays-new-terms-after-challenge-norway-media) that an Oslo court has since suspended the terms while it reviews them, and that Britain's competition regulator has opened its own investigation. Meta says the change is about pulling product information for ads, not scraping news sites.

What it means: courts are starting to decide whose work AI companies may take without paying for it. That matters for the quality of what you read, because news costs money to produce. Our piece on [who owns AI-generated content](/live-with-it/who-owns-ai-generated-content) explains the wider fight over [training data](/glossary/training-data).

## France's top book prize went to a novel with a ChatGPT sentence

Philippe Jaenada won the Prix Goncourt, France's most famous literary award, on 3 November for a novel about an unsolved murder in 1940s Paris. The next day he told France Inter radio that he had used AI "three times" while writing it, [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/04/goncourt-2026-winner-philippe-jaenada-admits-using-ai-in-novel-sparking-ai-debate-in-liter). He put in one sentence written by ChatGPT to "play with the reader", used it for technical research, and mocked its "completely absurd answers" on one point. "We cannot act as if it did not exist," he said, adding that writers must "tame it and not let ourselves be replaced."

It landed awkwardly. Weeks earlier another author's novel was dropped from the same prize's shortlist over suspected AI use, Euronews notes, and his publisher has stopped working with him.

What it means: there is no agreed line yet between using AI as a tool and letting it write for you, even among prize juries. If you use a chatbot for your own writing, being open about how you used it is the simplest rule. Our guide to [AI for everyday writing](/use/ai-for-everyday-writing) has practical tips.

## The US Justice Department sets up a "super intelligence" task force

The US Department of Justice announced on 5 November a Super Intelligence Task Force to organise criminal and civil cases involving the misuse of AI, [Nextgov reports](https://www.nextgov.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/11/department-justice-forms-ai-task-force/417074/). It takes over the work of an AI litigation unit set up in January and will work with a wider government group led by the national intelligence director, Jay Clayton. Nextgov notes it follows an executive order from President Trump in late September telling federal agencies to use the term "super intelligence" for AI.

What it means: in US government language, "super intelligence" now often means AI in general, not the far more capable future systems that researchers usually mean by [superintelligence](/glossary/superintelligence). When you see the word in a headline, check which meaning is in use. Our explainer on [SI, AI and AGI](/super-intelligence/si-ai-or-agi) sorts out the terms.

## Meta's AI assistant can now talk back, with a face

Meta added live voice calls and animated avatars to Muse, its personal AI assistant, [The Next Web reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/meta-muse-ai-voice-chat-animated-avatars) on 5 November. You can speak instead of typing, interrupt it mid-sentence, and choose from hundreds of voices, while an animated face reacts to what you say. It works in the Muse app on phones, the web and Mac, for now only in the US. Meta says voice calls will come to its smart glasses later, without a date.

What it means: a [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) with a voice and a face feels more like a person, which can make it easier to trust or get attached to. It is still the same software, and it can still be wrong. Our piece on [AI companions and loneliness](/live-with-it/ai-companions-and-loneliness) looks at what that closeness does to people.
