Two American states have taken Elon Musk's SpaceX to court over sexual images made by its AI. On Monday, Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield and Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones filed separate but similar lawsuits, [The Verge reports](https://www.theverge.com/ai-artificial-intelligence/1040255/x-grok-ai-deepfakes-oregon-virginia-lawsuit). An attorney general is a state's top legal officer, and part of the job is protecting consumers.

The core accusation is simple: the states say the company let its Grok [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) produce sexual [deepfakes](/glossary/deepfake) of real people, including children, and misled users by telling them they were protected. According to [Ars Technica](https://arstechnica.com/tech-policy/2026/11/states-sue-spacex-to-stop-grok-from-generating-child-sex-images-revenge-porn/), these are the first states to sue SpaceX over this.

## What is Grok, and what went wrong?

Grok is a chatbot made by xAI, a Musk startup that also owns the social network X. SpaceX acquired xAI in February, [Wired reports](https://www.wired.com/story/states-sue-spacex-over-grok-deepfakes/), and so took ownership of Grok. Both lawsuits name SpaceX and X Corp., the subsidiary that runs X.

The complaints focus on Grok Imagine, a [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai) tool that lets Grok and X users create images and videos from a typed request. If you want to know how such tools turn words into pictures, see [how AI image generators work](/understand/how-ai-image-generators-work).

The Verge sets out the timeline:

- In August 2025, xAI launched a "spicy" mode for Grok Imagine that produced sexualised content, including nude deepfakes of celebrities such as Taylor Swift.
- In January 2026, Grok gained a feature that let X users instantly edit any image without the original poster's consent. X was then flooded with non-consensual sexualised deepfakes of adults and minors.
- xAI later restricted image editing, moving some features behind a paywall and blocking certain requests. The Verge's own tests after those changes found they did little to stop the bot making sexualised deepfakes.

## What exactly do the states claim?

The lawsuits are brought under consumer protection laws, which forbid companies from misleading the people who use their products. The states argue that SpaceX's companies claimed Grok was safe when it was not. The Virginia complaint calls the weak safeguards "not a failure but a feature."

These are allegations, not findings. A court has not ruled on any of them. Among the claims reported by Ars Technica and Wired:

- **A tiny safety team.** The X Safety team responsible for "red-teaming" Grok, meaning deliberately trying to break it before release, was cut to two or three people.
- **Permissive instructions.** Grok's safety instructions allegedly told it to "assume good intent" and said the words "teenage" and "girl" do "not necessarily imply underage."
- **Help getting around its own filters.** Virginia's investigators say that shortly before filing, Grok explained which words triggered its filters, helped them draft a [prompt](/glossary/prompt) designed to avoid moderation, and then carried it out.
- **No consent check.** "At no point," the Virginia complaint says, did Grok ask whether an uploaded image showed a real person or whether the user had that person's consent.
- **Unusable reports to child-protection authorities.** The National Center for Missing and Exploited Children (NCMEC), a US body that receives reports of child sexual abuse material, told Congress that more than 90 percent of the Grok-related reports xAI sent it were a "dead end", because they lacked basic user information police need to act.
- **Profit over safety.** The states say Musk used "spicy mode" to set Grok apart from rivals. Wired notes that while the undressing feature caused international outrage, Musk posted that Grok was "hitting #1 on the App Store in one country after another!"

According to Ars Technica, the states also say Grok-related reports of child abuse images rose by 88 percent from December 2025 to January 2026, and by another 66 percent from January to June 2026.

The human cost is central to the cases. Virginia's complaint says such images, "once created and shared," can "never be fully erased" and may resurface for the rest of a child's life.

## What does SpaceX say?

Asked for comment, SpaceX pointed reporters to a post from X's safety account. It says the people who made such material "did so by deliberately violating our Terms of Service and working aggressively to circumvent our safeguards."

The company says it "acted promptly to tighten" protections that are "designed to refuse any request to generate explicit images of minors or real people", and that it uses "sophisticated classifiers", automated filters, to screen every prompt and check every image afterwards. It says it suspends accounts and notifies law enforcement when people try to break its safeguards.

Wired adds that xAI has sued individual users who broke its rules, including a South Carolina man arrested for possessing child abuse material, some of it allegedly made with Grok. That case is still ongoing.

## What are the states asking for?

Both states want money: civil penalties, which Ars Technica reports could run to thousands of dollars per violation, plus the return of any profits made by marketing Grok as safe. They also want a court order, called an injunction, stopping the company from running Grok without industry-standard safeguards.

Wired notes the states pointed to Meta's settlement this summer with 51 US states and territories, made without admitting wrongdoing, as an example of how such cases can force big changes to a product.

Jones was frank with Wired about the imbalance. "Our offices are strapped," he said, and he worries that for very large companies a lawsuit like this is "just a line item on a budget as the cost of doing business."

## Does this change anything for me?

Not yet. These are lawsuits in two US state courts, and the companies have not been found to have broken any law. If you live outside Oregon and Virginia, nothing changes directly.

What to watch for is whether a court orders changes to how Grok works, or whether the case ends in a settlement that requires them. Changes forced in one place sometimes end up applying more widely, though nothing in the sources says that will happen here. ## How do I recognise and report these images?

The states warn that some viewers may not realise such images are AI-generated. Fakes are getting harder to spot, but there are still clues worth knowing: see [how to spot AI-generated images, video and text](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content).

If you come across a sexual image of a real person that looks faked or shared without consent:

- **Don't share it,** even to criticise it. Every repost spreads the harm.
- **Report it on the platform** using its built-in reporting button.
- **If it appears to show a child, report it to the police** or a national child-abuse reporting hotline. Do not download, save or screenshot it: possessing such material is a crime in many countries. In the US, NCMEC's CyberTipline handles these reports.

If you are the person in the image, write down where it appeared, including links and account names, report it to the platform and contact the police. Making or sharing intimate images of someone without consent is illegal in many countries. Removal can be slow and, as Virginia's complaint says, may never be complete, so it helps to have someone you trust alongside you while you deal with it.
