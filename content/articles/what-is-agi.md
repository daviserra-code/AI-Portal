[AGI](/glossary/agi) stands for artificial general intelligence. Most people who use it mean an AI that can do most of the thinking work a capable adult can, across many different fields, instead of being brilliant at one thing and lost at the next.

That sounds simple. The trouble is that nobody agrees where "most" begins, which tasks count, or how to check. So in September 2026, when OpenAI launched a model called GPT-6 Astra, its president said "we are now in the AGI era", Nvidia's chief executive posted "AGI has arrived", and a well-known critic replied that "we are nowhere near AGI." All three were talking about the same software.

AGI sits one rung below [superintelligence](/glossary/superintelligence), which means AI far beyond the best humans at almost everything. We cover that idea, and who is worried about it, in [What is superintelligence?](/super-intelligence/what-is-superintelligence). This piece is about the rung below, and about the harder question: how anyone would know it had been reached.

## Why is AGI so hard to define?

Because "general intelligence" is hard to define in people, too. Different definitions pick out different things, and each one leads to a different answer about whether AGI is here.

A few of the main ones in use today:

- **Economic.** OpenAI's charter defines AGI as "highly autonomous systems that outperform humans at most economically valuable work." By this measure, AGI is about jobs: can the system do the work people are paid for?
- **Cognitive.** A 2025 paper by more than 30 researchers, including Dan Hendrycks, Yoshua Bengio and the AI critic Gary Marcus, defines AGI as "an AI that can match or exceed the cognitive versatility and proficiency of a well-educated adult." It borrows a model psychologists use to describe human intelligence and splits it into ten areas, such as knowledge, reasoning and memory.
- **Learning.** The ARC Prize Foundation, which runs one of the best-known AGI tests, says "AGI is a system that can match the learning efficiency of humans." What counts here is not what the system knows but how quickly it picks up something new.
- **Discovery.** Demis Hassabis, who runs Google DeepMind, has suggested a much higher bar: train a system only on knowledge up to 1911 and see whether it can work out Einstein's theory of general relativity, which came in 1915, by itself. Today's systems clearly could not, he said at the AI Impact Summit in Delhi, as reported by AI Magazine.

Some industry leaders have given up on the word. Sam Altman, OpenAI's chief executive, called AGI "not a super-useful term" in a CNBC appearance in 2025, Fortune reported. Dario Amodei, who runs Anthropic, told CNBC's Squawk Box, according to Business Insider, "I've always thought of it as a marketing term." He prefers to talk about "powerful AI", or a "country of geniuses in a data center." (Anthropic makes the AI that helped draft this article.)

## Why do companies care so much about the word?

Partly because it was written into contracts. For years, Microsoft's deal with OpenAI included a clause tied to OpenAI reaching AGI. In October 2025 the two companies said that "once AGI is declared by OpenAI, that declaration will now be verified by an independent expert panel."

In April 2026 the deal changed again. Microsoft's announcement says OpenAI's revenue-share payments to Microsoft now continue through 2030 "independent of OpenAI's technology progress", and it does not mention AGI at all. The programmer and writer Simon Willison read that as the end of the AGI clause. Whatever the legal detail, it shows that for the companies, "AGI" has been as much a business term as a scientific one.

## How do researchers try to measure it?

There is no single AGI test. There are frameworks for sorting systems into levels, and benchmarks that probe particular abilities.

**Levels, not a finish line.** In 2023 researchers at Google DeepMind proposed "Levels of AGI", which grades systems on how good they are and how broad. The performance levels run from "Emerging" ("equal to or somewhat better than an unskilled human") through "Competent" (at least the 50th percentile of skilled adults), "Expert" (90th), "Virtuoso" (99th) and finally "Superhuman" ("outperforms 100% of humans"). At the time, the paper put ChatGPT, Bard, Llama 2 and Gemini at the first rung, "Emerging AGI". One of its principles is to "focus on the path to AGI rather than a single endpoint."

**A score out of 100.** The 2025 definition paper turns its ten areas into a percentage. It scored GPT-4 at 27% and GPT-5 at 57%. The authors describe a "highly 'jagged'" profile: strong on knowledge, with "critical deficits" in basics such as long-term memory.

**Puzzles that can't be memorised.** The ARC benchmarks, started by the AI researcher François Chollet in 2019, test whether a system can solve new puzzles it has never seen, using only basic common sense about shapes, colours and counting. The latest version, ARC-AGI-3, launched in March 2026, drops the system into small game-like worlds with no instructions. It has to work out the goal and the rules by playing. In testing, each game was tried by ten members of the public, and every game in the set was solved by people. Frontier AI systems scored below 1% at launch, according to the foundation's paper.

## So has any system passed?

On one test, nearly. On 3 September 2026 the ARC Prize Foundation reported that GPT-6 Astra scored 62.7% on ARC-AGI-3's held-back games using the foundation's standard setup, and 99.9% using a setup built around OpenAI's own features. In the second case it needed fewer moves than the typical human player on 96% of levels.

That gap between 62.7% and 99.9% is part of the story. The "harness", the software wrapped around an AI model that decides what it remembers between moves and how it sees the screen, can change scores dramatically. Tech Times reports that in an OpenAI experiment the same earlier model went from 7.8% to 38.3% just by changing two such settings. The foundation keeps a standard harness so models can be compared fairly.

Even the test's makers say a high score does not settle the question. The foundation's post on Astra says "we are not claiming that it is AGI." Chollet told Fast Company that solving the benchmark is "a strong sign of progress" but "it is not proof of AGI, and that was never the point." He has also said, according to The Decoder, that progress is running faster than he expected and that AGI may come sooner than his earlier guess of around 2030.

Other measures disagree with each other. The Decoder reports that Epoch AI's capability index ranked Astra first of 267 models, while Artificial Analysis gave it the same score as OpenAI's previous model and placed it behind a rival.

## What do the big labs say now?

These are claims by the companies and their leaders, not measured facts.

- **OpenAI.** According to Gizmodo, the company stopped short of formally declaring AGI when it launched GPT-6 Astra, but its president Greg Brockman said "it's not unreasonable to feel that we are now in the AGI era." In a TIME interview in August 2026, as reported by GIGAZINE, Altman said "by the end of 2026, we will have an internal system that we call AGI."
- **Nvidia.** Jensen Huang, whose company sells the chips the model was trained on, posted "AGI has arrived" after the launch, Fox Business reports.
- **Google DeepMind.** Hassabis has said "I don't think we are there yet." He names three gaps: systems that keep learning from experience, planning over months or years, and consistency. Models that solve Olympiad-level maths can still fail simple questions phrased differently, and "a true general intelligence system shouldn't have that kind of jaggedness," he said.
- **Anthropic.** Amodei avoids the term, and, as Fortune reported in 2025, said "powerful AI" could arrive by 2027.

Independent researchers are more cautious. Gary Marcus told Fast Company the AGI claims are "marketing by people who either don't know the original definitions or are deliberately lowering the bar." Ben Goertzel, one of the researchers who shaped the term in the early 2000s, called Astra "superhuman at some things and subhuman at others." In a 2025 survey run for the AAAI, a large association of AI researchers, 76% of respondents said simply scaling up today's approaches was "unlikely" or "very unlikely" to produce AGI, The Decoder reported.

## Why might there be no single moment it "arrives"?

Because the abilities don't move together. Today's systems can beat most people at some coding and maths tasks and still stumble over things a child manages, which is what researchers mean by "jagged". A system can be "Expert" on one ladder and "Emerging" on another at the same time.

Because the line moves depending on who draws it. On OpenAI's economic definition, AGI could be declared by a company and checked by a panel. On Hassabis's discovery test, it is nowhere close. On ARC-AGI-3, the result depends partly on the software around the model.

And because tests get used up. Once a benchmark is close to solved, its makers build a harder one, as ARC has done three times. That is useful for tracking progress, but it means a passed test rarely ends the argument.

That is why the Google DeepMind framework talks about a path instead of an endpoint. AGI is more likely to show up as a long stretch in which AI systems quietly take on more kinds of work, than as a single headline day.

## What does this mean for you?

Not much changes the day someone says "AGI". The AI tools you use today, such as [chatbots](/glossary/chatbot) and [AI agents](/understand/ai-agents-explained), keep their strengths and their weak spots, including confident mistakes known as [hallucinations](/glossary/hallucination).

When you see an AGI claim, three questions help. Who is making it, and do they gain from it? Which definition are they using? And which test, under which setup? If the answers are missing, treat the claim as an opinion. For why some researchers think the stakes are high whatever the label, see [What is AI safety?](/super-intelligence/what-is-ai-safety).
