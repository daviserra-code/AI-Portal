The photo app on a recent phone is now a small AI editing studio. It can erase a stranger from a beach shot, swap a grey background, sharpen a far-off detail and find "the picture of Mum with the birthday cake" without you scrolling. Most of this runs on [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai): the app does not just adjust what is there, it paints in new pixels it thinks belong.

That is the key thing to understand. A crop or a brightness slider changes how a photo looks. A generative edit can change what the photo shows. Both have their place, and the rest of this guide is about knowing which one you are doing.

## What can the built-in editors do?

The main tools are much the same across brands, even if the names differ.

- **Remove things.** Apple calls it Clean Up, Google has Magic Eraser, and Samsung's version sits under Generative photo editing. You brush or circle the unwanted object and the app fills the gap. Apple's help page notes that Clean Up may highlight likely distractions for you, and that brushing over a face blurs it with a pixelated effect instead of erasing it.
- **Move, resize or expand.** Samsung's support page says Generative edit lets you move, delete or resize objects, and zoom out past the edges so the AI fills in a wider scene. Apple's tools include Extend and Spatial Reframing for similar jobs.
- **Edit by asking.** Google Photos lets you type or say what you want. In its August 2025 announcement, Google gave examples such as "remove the cars in the background", "remove the reflections and fix the washed out colors" and simply "make it better", plus playful changes like a new background or a party hat. The feature started on the Pixel 10 in the US. In June 2026, 9to5Google reported it reaching Android users in Germany, the UK, France, Spain and Italy.
- **Sharpen distant shots.** Google's Pixel 10 Pro has a Pro Zoom mode that goes up to 100x. Google's own page says it uses "powerful new generative models" to rebuild the distant image from several frames. In plain terms, beyond a certain point the phone is guessing at detail it could not actually see.

The same caution applies to the clever tricks as to any [model](/glossary/model): if it does not have the information, it fills the gap with something plausible. Our explainer on [how AI image generators work](/understand/how-ai-image-generators-work) shows why.

### Prompts that tend to work

If your app takes plain-language requests, be specific, and ask for one change at a time so you can undo it cleanly. Our guide to [writing a good prompt](/use/how-to-write-a-good-prompt) covers the general habits. For photos, try:

- "Remove the person walking behind us on the left, keep everything else the same."
- "Fix the colours, the photo looks too yellow from the indoor lights."
- "Remove the glare on the window."
- "Straighten the horizon and crop out the bin on the right."
- "Restore this old photo: remove scratches and dust, but don't change anyone's face."

That last instruction matters, as the next section explains.

## Can AI restore old family photos?

Yes, and it can be lovely: tears, dust spots and fading are exactly the kind of damage these tools handle well. Google lists "restore this old photo" as one of its sample requests.

Faces are the tricky part. Back in 2022, the researchers behind a free restoration tool called GFP-GAN warned of a "slight change of identity" in the faces it restored, Engadget reported. In a 2026 test of a newer product, ON1 Restore AI, PetaPixel found it added hair, make-up and "enhanced" features that were not in the originals, and in one group photo changed people so much that they were "unlikely" to be recognisable as family members. ON1 told PetaPixel the results were early and that it was re-evaluating them.

So keep the original scan, always. Treat the restored version as a reworked copy, and if you share it with relatives or a family-history site, say it was AI-restored.

## How does AI search and sorting work?

Photo apps now understand what is in your pictures, so you can search the way you would describe a photo to a friend.

On iPhone, Apple's Photos app has accepted natural-language searches since iOS 18.1, such as a named person "smiling" or a scene plus an activity, and it can jump to the right moment inside a video, MacRumors explains. The library is indexed on the phone itself. A separate setting, Enhanced Visual Search, finds landmarks; Apple says it "works without sending your photos or videos to Apple", sending only an encrypted mathematical summary that Apple's servers "can't decrypt". You can turn it off under **Settings > Apps > Photos**.

In Google Photos, the feature is called Ask Photos and runs on Google's Gemini. Google's help page gives examples like "What did I eat on my trip to Barcelona?" It reached Germany, Austria and Switzerland in March 2026, GoogleWatchBlog reported. To use it you have to confirm which face group is you, and it only works on backed-up photos. Google says humans don't review Ask Photos responses "unless you give feedback, or in rare cases to address abuse or harm", but your queries may be reviewed after privacy protections are applied. You can stop that under **Gemini features in Photos > Help us improve photos**, or turn Ask Photos off entirely in the same menu.

## Will people be able to tell I used AI?

Often, yes, if they look in the right place. The big three now tag AI edits in different ways.

**Google.** Pixel 10 phones attach Content Credentials, the industry standard also known as C2PA, to every photo taken with the Pixel camera, Google says. Google Photos adds them when you save an AI edit. Open a photo, tap **More**, then **About**, and scroll to "How this was made". You may see "Edited with AI tools" when generative AI created new pixels, or "Edited with non-AI tools" for a crop or rotation.

**Apple.** Since iOS 27.2, Apple says Clean Up, Extend, Spatial Reframing, Image Playground and Image Wand add an invisible SynthID watermark, a marker developed by Google that is woven into the pixels themselves. You can check an image at synthid.com. MacRumors found Clean Up inconsistent in its tests: small edits carried no watermark, larger ones did.

**Samsung.** Samsung's help page says outputs of Generative photo editing "are watermarked to indicate edits". Adobe said the Galaxy S25 would be the first phone line to support Content Credentials, TechCrunch reported.

None of this is airtight. Android Authority stripped all the C2PA data from an AI-edited Pixel photo with one free command-line tool, and a screenshot loses metadata too. A missing label proves nothing either way. Our guide to [spotting AI-generated content](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content) explains how to read these labels on other people's pictures.

## Is face recognition in photo apps private?

It depends on where the work happens, and in Europe it touches some of the strictest data rules there are.

EU data protection law treats "biometric data processed solely to identify a human being" as sensitive data, which gets extra protection, according to the European Commission. Grouping faces by person is a version of that, so it pays to know your settings.

- **Apple** says Photos "uses on-device analysis to recognise the faces of people in photos", and that it "does not access your photos or videos". Names you add sync to your other devices through iCloud.
- **Google Photos** creates face models to sort your pictures into face groups. Its help page says these "may be considered biometric data in some jurisdictions", and that the feature isn't available in every country. Turning off **Face Groups** in Photos settings deletes your face groups, face models and labels; otherwise Google keeps them until you delete them or your account is inactive for more than two years.
- **Meta** shut down Facebook's old face recognition system after public backlash, Engadget notes, but since 2025 it has used facial recognition in the EU, UK and South Korea to help people recover hacked accounts and, for public figures who opt in, to catch impostor accounts.

Remember that the people in your photos haven't agreed to any of this. If a friend asks you not to tag or label them, that is a reasonable request to honour.

## What is fair to change?

For your own albums, almost anything goes. Removing a bin from a holiday snap hurts nobody. EU law agrees: the Commission's guidance on the [AI Act](/glossary/ai-act) says its rules on labelling AI-made images exclude "personal, non-professional activities", though anyone earning money from such content on a regular basis counts as a professional. Our [EU AI Act explainer](/live-with-it/eu-ai-act-explained) covers what professionals must disclose.

The line moves when someone else relies on the photo being true. A useful test: would the person looking at it make a different decision if they knew what you changed?

- **News and evidence.** The Associated Press says it does "not alter any elements" of its photos and does "not allow the use of generative AI to add or subtract any elements." Insurance claims, rental deposit photos and anything you might hand to a court fall in the same group: don't edit them at all.
- **ID photos.** No AI edits, no beauty filters. The UK requires digital passport photos to be "unaltered by computer software". Germany went further: since 1 May 2025, ID and passport photos must be taken at the office or sent digitally by a certified photographer, the consumer group Verbraucherzentrale reports. The government's stated reason is "face morphing", where two people's faces are blended into one picture so that both could use the same document.
- **Dating profiles.** Fixing the lighting is fine; changing your body, age or life is not. CBS News reported in October 2026 that Hinge, Bumble and Match Group all have policies against misleading AI images, and that one man was banned from Hinge over an AI-generated rooftop photo. Bumble has let members report profiles "using AI-generated photos or videos" since 2024.
- **Competitions.** Read the rules before you enter. In 2023 the German artist Boris Eldagsen won a Sony World Photography Award with an AI-generated image, then refused it, saying "AI is not photography." World Press Photo now prohibits generative fill and fully generated images in all its categories.

If you're unsure, the honest fix is cheap: say what you changed. A caption like "background cleaned up with AI" costs nothing.
