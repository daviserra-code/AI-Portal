Meta's new assistant, Muse, became available in the UK on 24 November 2026, [the BBC reports](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/c9gkld2l38y6o?at_medium=RSS&at_campaign=rss). It was already out in the US, Canada and Australia. According to [the Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/nov/24/have-you-downloaded-metas-ai-assistant-muse-or-do-you-plan-to), the app has been a hit in the US, ranking first on Google's free app store and second on Apple's.

Muse is what the tech industry calls an [AI agent](/glossary/ai-agent). An ordinary [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) answers your questions. An agent goes a step further and does things for you: visiting websites, filling in forms, sending messages. Our guide to [AI agents](/understand/ai-agents-explained) explains the idea in more detail.

## What can Muse actually do?

Meta's own examples, quoted by the BBC, are "searching for a cheaper energy deal" and "sorting the school calendar". The Guardian describes it as a digital butler that can go shopping, book tickets and carry out negotiations on your behalf.

You give it a job, such as finding some holiday options, and it keeps working after you close the app, then reports back when it is done. "It doesn't just answer questions, it actually goes out and does work for you," David Singleton, Meta's VP of engineering on AI products, told the BBC.

It also comes with a personality. The default is a cuddly avatar, but you can change its name, look and character. BBC technology editor Zoe Kleinman, who tested it for a week, called hers Linda and gave it a British female voice.

It can be pushy. Kleinman says it summarised every incoming email and suggested a reply, sometimes long before she was ready to answer.

## Where can it shop for me?

In the UK, Meta has signed deals with 14 businesses so that Muse can find its way around their websites more easily, the Guardian reports. Firms named as partners across the two reports include:

- Marks & Spencer
- Sainsbury's
- Ocado
- Currys
- Debenhams Group
- easyJet
- Trainline

Outside those partners, it can struggle. Amazon has blocked Muse from its marketplace, citing data security concerns, according to the BBC. Kleinman's agent didn't seem to know this and spent an hour telling her it was waiting for a page to load.

It also found her some train tickets but couldn't book them through the cheaper app she normally uses, so it told her to book them herself. If you want to compare prices on your own terms, see our guide to [using AI to shop smarter](/use/ai-for-shopping).

## Will it spend my money without asking?

Not by default. Meta says Muse cannot make a purchase or send an email without your permission. Kleinman found the requests "keep on coming": every time Muse wants to do, send or post something, you have to approve it.

You can cut down those requests by giving it more freedom in the settings. That is a real trade-off. The UK's data watchdog, the Information Commissioner's Office (ICO), warned in a blog post timed for the launch that "agents can make mistakes. Mistakes can be magnified if the agent is acting autonomously."

The mistakes aren't only about money. Kleinman says Muse wrote some embarrassing replies in her name, such as answering a simple invitation with a full list of travel logistics when "yes, that sounds great" would have done.

## What about my privacy?

Muse is most useful when it can see your most personal accounts. Kleinman connected a personal email account, her personal calendar and her phone messages, and says she found herself "wincing" as she did it. It can't read your WhatsApp chats: there, it can only set up a separate conversation between you and Muse.

The ICO's chief executive, Paul Arnold, said the regulator had been "clear with Meta about its responsibilities under data protection law prior to launch", particularly the need for people to "meaningfully understand and control how their personal data is used". Kleinman points out that Meta is asking for this trust despite what she calls "a rather dubious track record" with its social networks.

If you decide to try it, a few sensible habits:

- Connect one account first, not all of them, and see what Muse does with it.
- Keep the permission prompts on until you trust its judgement.
- Read every message or order it prepares before you approve it.
- Check what each connection lets it see, and how you would remove that access later.

Our guide to [sharing your data with chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) covers the wider questions.

## Is it free, and is it coming to Europe?

You have to be 18 or over. Muse is free up to a certain point, after which subscriptions apply, according to the BBC. Neither report gives prices or says where that limit sits.

Kleinman also noticed Muse nudging her when she hadn't used it for a while, with prompts like "did you know you can change my outfit?" She suspects these are designed to keep people using it.

The reports cover the US, UK, Canada and Australia only. Neither says whether or when Muse will reach the EU.

## Why is Meta pushing this so hard?

Meta is spending $145bn (£111bn) this year, much of it on AI, the BBC reports, and hopes Muse will do for AI agents what WhatsApp did for messaging.

The day before the UK launch, Meta's head of AI, Alexandr Wang, published an essay warning how hard it will be for humans to stay in control once AI is 1,000 times more intelligent than us. That is a claim about a possible future [superintelligence](/glossary/superintelligence), not a description of Muse, which for now still asks before it sends your emails.
