## What has AI actually done in elections so far?

Quite a lot of mischief, and a few cases that made history. The best documented ones fall into three groups: fake audio and video of candidates, cloned voices on the phone, and networks of fake accounts.

**A fake president on the phone.** Two days before the New Hampshire primary in January 2024, thousands of Democrats got a robocall in an AI-generated voice resembling Joe Biden, telling them to save their vote for November, [CBS News reports](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/boston/news/new-hampshire-jury-acquits-consultant-ai-robocalls-biden). The political consultant behind it, Steven Kramer, was fined $6 million by the US Federal Communications Commission and told the AP he would not pay. The phone company that carried the calls agreed to a $1 million settlement. In June 2025 a New Hampshire jury acquitted Kramer of all voter suppression and impersonation charges.

**A fake recording two days before the vote.** In Slovakia's 2023 election, a fake audio clip appeared to capture the pro-European leader Michal Šimečka discussing vote rigging with a journalist. Both denied it was real. It spread during the country's pre-election media silence, when news outlets could not easily debunk it, according to a [study in the HKS Misinformation Review](https://misinforeview.hks.harvard.edu/article/beyond-the-deepfake-hype-ai-democracy-and-the-slovak-case/). Šimečka had led the polls and lost to Robert Fico.

**A fake news bulletin.** In October 2025, days before Ireland's presidential election, a video styled as an RTÉ news report showed AI-made versions of a presenter and of candidate Catherine Connolly announcing she had pulled out. Connolly called it a "malicious deepfake", the [Irish Examiner reports](https://www.irishexaminer.com/news/politics/arid-+41728776.html). Meta removed it after the Electoral Commission complained, though it stayed visible on X for a time.

**Fake crowds.** In December 2024 Romania's constitutional court annulled the first round of its presidential election, after declassified intelligence alleged a coordinated campaign of thousands of social media accounts, on TikTok and Telegram among others, promoting the far-right candidate Călin Georgescu, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2024/12/6/romanias-top-court-annuls-results-of-presidential-elections-first-round). That case was about coordinated accounts more than about AI-made content, but it shows how seriously courts can now take online manipulation. Separately, OpenAI said in August 2024 that it had banned ChatGPT accounts run by an Iranian operation that wrote fake news articles and social posts about the US election. It added that the posts got almost no engagement, [as OPB reported](https://www.opb.org/article/2024/08/17/openai-says-iranian-group-using-chatgpt-tried-to-sow-division-ahead-of-u-s-election).

## What is happening in the US midterms?

The US midterm elections are on 3 November 2026, and this is the first big campaign in which the candidates themselves use AI ads at scale. The Wesleyan Media Project counted 220 political ads made at least partly with AI across 34 states, with more than $137 million spent on them, [Gizmodo reported](https://gizmodo.com/137-million-has-been-spent-on-ai-generated-ads-ahead-of-midterms-only-27-included-a-disclaimer-2000827138) on 16 October. In the 2024 cycle the same researchers found less than $50,000 of such spending. Only 27% of the ads carried a disclaimer, and nearly half featured [deepfakes](/glossary/deepfake) of real politicians.

Many are not trying to fool anyone. One expert at New York University calls the style "slopaganda": obviously fake scenes meant to make a point, such as a rival shown as a superhero or a party guest who won't leave, [Al Jazeera reports](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2026/10/9/ai-deepfake-ads-grow-more-popular-in-us-midterm-campaigns-blurring-truth). The line between satire and deception is exactly where the fights happen. New York Democrats have complained to the state's election board that ads by Republican governor candidate Bruce Blakeman, showing opponents in scenes that never happened, break the state's disclosure law. His campaign says satire needs no label.

## What do platforms and laws require?

It depends on where you live, and on whether the content is a paid ad or an ordinary post.

**Platforms.** Google has required election advertisers since 2023 to disclose AI-generated or digitally altered material in ads on Google and YouTube, [CNBC reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2023/09/07/google-will-soon-require-disclosures-for-ai-generated-election-ads-.html). Meta asks advertisers to flag political ads in which a realistic person says or does something they didn't, or an event appears that never happened, [according to TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2023/11/08/meta-will-start-requiring-disclosures-for-political-ads-manipulated-with-ai). Minor edits are exempt. These rules depend on advertisers being honest, which is why so few midterm ads carry a label.

**The United States.** There is no federal law on AI deepfakes in election ads, Al Jazeera notes. The states fill the gap: about 30 have their own election deepfake laws, depending on how you count, according to [Public Citizen's tracker](https://www.citizen.org/article/tracker-legislation-on-deepfakes-in-elections/), which lists one law struck down in court and another that has expired. Most of these laws require AI-made political ads to be labelled, Al Jazeera says. On the phone, the FCC ruled unanimously on 8 February 2024 that AI-generated voices count as "artificial" under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, so robocalls using them need prior consent, [law firm Akin Gump explains](https://www.akingump.com/en/insights/ai-law-and-regulation-tracker/fcc-issues-declaratory-ruling-on-ai-generated-voice-robocalls). We cover voice cloning in more depth in our guide to [AI voice clone scams](/live-with-it/ai-voice-clone-scams).

**The European Union.** Three sets of rules overlap:

- Under the Digital Services Act, the largest platforms must manage election risks. Guidelines the European Commission adopted in April 2024 ask them to clearly label AI-generated content such as deepfakes, or prove that what they do instead works as well, [according to Lewis Silkin](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2024/05/31/european-commission-publishes-guidelines-under-the-dsa-to-protect-the-integrity-o-102j8xw).
- Article 50 of the [AI Act](/glossary/ai-act), in force since 2 August 2026, requires anyone who publishes a deepfake to disclose it, with a lighter duty for obvious satire. Makers of AI generators must also mark their output so software can detect it. Tools already on the market before August have until 2 December 2026 for that marking, [Garrigues reports](https://www.garrigues.com/en_GB/garrigues-digital/ai-digital-omnibus-regulation-has-been-published-redefining-deadlines-and).
- An EU regulation on political advertising has applied since 10 October 2025, requiring labels and disclosure of who paid. Meta and Google chose to stop selling political ads in the EU rather than comply, [Al Jazeera reported](https://www.aljazeera.com/economy/2025/7/25/meta-to-suspend-political-advertising-in-the-eu-as-transparency-law-looms).

For how these rules compare with Britain's and China's, see [who regulates AI](/super-intelligence/who-regulates-ai).

## Does AI disinformation actually change votes?

So far, the evidence says rarely, if ever, in a way anyone can prove. After the 2024 UK, French and European Parliament elections, researchers at the Alan Turing Institute found only 16 viral cases of AI disinformation in the UK campaign and 11 across the French and EU votes, and concluded they "did not meaningfully impact election results", [the IET reported](https://engx.theiet.org/b/articles/posts/researchers-find-no-evidence-that-ai-generated-content-impacted-recent-european-elections).

Even the Slovak case is less clear than its reputation. The authors of the Harvard study call the claim that a deepfake swung that election "reductive", pointing to low trust in institutions, years of pro-Kremlin messaging and polls that may have undercounted Fico's voters.

The harm is real but slower: fakes give people a reason to doubt everything, including genuine recordings, and they waste the time of election officials and journalists.

The newer worry is conversation, not video. In two studies published in December 2025 in Nature and Science, researchers had people chat with AI [chatbots](/glossary/chatbot) arguing for a candidate or a position. In the US, a short chat moved people toward the candidate they opposed by 2 to 4 points on a 100-point scale, roughly four times the measured effect of political ads in the 2016 and 2020 US elections. In Canada and Poland, opposing voters moved by about 10 points, [MIT Technology Review reports](https://www.technologyreview.com/2025/12/04/1128824/ai-chatbots-can-sway-voters-better-than-political-advertisements/). The UK study, with nearly 77,000 participants, found that the most persuasive models packed their answers with claims and were also the least accurate, [Oxford University says](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2025-12-11-study-reveals-how-conversational-ai-can-exert-influence-over-political-beliefs). Participants knew they were talking to a persuader, so real-world effects may be smaller.

## Can I ask a chatbot how or where to vote?

You can, but check the answer. Chatbots are trained on old data and can make things up, a problem known as [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination). In February 2024 a test by AI Democracy Projects and Proof News found five leading chatbots gave inaccurate election answers more than half the time, including telling Californians they could vote by text message, [CBS News reported](https://cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/ai-chatbots-inaccurate-election-information-proof-news).

Things have improved but not enough. In September 2026 the Institute for Strategic Dialogue asked six chatbots common voting questions for ten US states and found 29% of answers incomplete, inaccurate or outdated, including wrong election dates, [The Hill reported](https://www.abc27.com/national/nearly-a-third-of-ai-chatbot-replies-to-voting-questions-inaccurate-outdated-study/amp/). Accuracy ranged from 61% to 89% depending on the chatbot, and dropped when questions were asked in Spanish.

Asking a chatbot who to vote for is a different problem: [bias](/glossary/bias). Before the Dutch election in October 2025, the country's data protection authority gave four chatbots 200 voter profiles per party. In 56% of cases they pointed to one of the two parties expected to win most seats, and centrist parties were almost never suggested, [Euronews reported](https://www.euronews.com/2025/10/29/fact-check-can-you-rely-on-ai-chatbots-for-voting-advice). A project manager at the authority called it "a kind of oversimplification" of Dutch politics.

For dates, deadlines, ID rules and polling places, go to your official election office's website. Google's Gemini app gives the same advice in its own disclaimer: "Election info changes quickly. Verify responses with official sources."

## How do I check a clip or claim before sharing it?

Our guide to [spotting AI-generated content](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content) explains the general techniques, such as reverse image search and content credentials. For election material, a few extra habits help:

1. **Notice the timing.** A shocking clip in the last days before a vote, especially during a media blackout, is the classic pattern from Slovakia and Ireland. Be most careful then.
2. **Find the original.** Who posted it first? An account named after a broadcaster is not the broadcaster. Check the outlet's own website or app.
3. **Look for the candidate's response.** Campaigns deny fakes quickly. If a candidate "quits", their own channels will say so.
4. **Check reputable news and fact-checkers.** A real scandal will be reported by several established outlets within hours.
5. **Be suspicious of voting-process claims.** Messages saying the vote is cancelled, moved, or possible by text are almost always false. Check with your election office.
6. **Don't trust a voice alone.** A familiar voice on a robocall proves nothing.
7. **Look for a label, but don't rely on its absence.** Most AI ads in the US midterms had none.
8. **When unsure, don't share.** Report it to the platform instead, as Connolly asked her supporters to do.
