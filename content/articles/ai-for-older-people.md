Most older people in Europe have not tried the new AI tools yet. According to Eurostat, 7% of people aged 65 to 74 in the EU used a [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai) tool such as ChatGPT in the three months before they were asked in 2025, against 64% of people aged 16 to 24. In the US the picture is moving faster: AARP's 2026 tech survey found that 30% of adults aged 50 and over used AI in 2025, up from 18% the year before.

So if you feel you have missed something, you are in large company. The good news is that the most useful AI for older people is often not a chatbot at all. It is already sitting inside the phone in your pocket, under a name like "Live Caption" or "Magnifier".

## What can AI actually help with?

[Artificial intelligence](/glossary/artificial-intelligence) here simply means software that has learned from huge amounts of examples to recognise speech, pictures or text, and to produce its own. That makes it good at a few things that get harder with age: hearing, seeing, and wading through paperwork.

**Hearing conversations and calls.** Android phones have a feature called Live Caption that writes out speech as it happens, including phone calls on Pixel and selected other Android phones. Google says the captions are "processed locally, never stored, and never leave your device." To turn it on, press a volume button, tap More in the volume panel, choose Live Caption and tap Done. If that doesn't work, search for "Live Caption" in Settings. iPhones have a similar feature called Live Captions, which Apple says works for calls, media and conversations in the room.

Hearing aids are changing too. In September 2024, the US Food and Drug Administration authorised software that turns compatible Apple AirPods Pro earbuds into an over-the-counter hearing aid for adults with "perceived mild to moderate hearing impairment", set up from an iPhone without a hearing professional. That decision covers the US; check with your audiologist or pharmacist what is available where you live, and treat it as a first step, not a replacement for a hearing test.

**Reading small print and seeing what is around you.** Point your phone camera at a menu, a medicine label or a letter and AI can enlarge it or read it out loud. Apple describes its Magnifier app as working "like a digital magnifying glass". For people who are blind or have low vision, the Be My Eyes app includes Be My AI, which describes photos of your surroundings, reads labels and helps with menus and documents. Be My Eyes says it is free, and that human volunteers are on hand "for tougher requests."

**Explaining letters, forms and everyday questions.** A [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) can turn a dense letter from a pension office or energy company into plain words, draft a polite reply, or explain a word you don't know. It is patient, available at any hour, and never minds being asked the same thing twice. Our guide to [AI for everyday writing](/use/ai-for-everyday-writing) shows how to ask well.

**Staying organised and safe.** Age UK points to apps that remind you of appointments and medication, and to voice assistants that can read things aloud or call a family member hands-free. Apple's Health app can log medications and remind you to take them, and an Apple Watch can call emergency services after a hard fall if you don't respond. Apple also offers Assistive Access, a simpler iPhone layout with bigger buttons and only the apps you choose.

**Company.** Some people chat to AI for company. The research on whether that helps is mixed; our piece on [AI companions and loneliness](/live-with-it/ai-companions-and-loneliness) sets out what is known.

## What should I be careful about?

AI is a helpful assistant, not an expert, and three habits keep it that way.

- **Check anything important.** Chatbots can state wrong things with total confidence, a problem known as [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination). Before you act on advice about money, law or medicine, check it with the organisation itself or a professional. Our guide to [AI and health questions](/live-with-it/ai-and-health-questions) explains where chatbots help with health and where they don't.
- **Keep private things private.** Age UK's advice is never to give passwords, bank account numbers or contact details to public AI tools. Our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) shows how to stop chats being used for training and how to delete them.
- **Remember it is a machine.** Age UK puts it well: chatbots "don't have emotions or opinions, even though they're designed to seem like they do."

Worries are common and reasonable. In AARP's survey, trust, privacy and data security were the top concerns of about half of older adults who were hesitant about new technology, and three in five adults over 50 said technology is not designed with their age in mind. Age UK also stresses that using AI is "for the most part optional".

## Which scams use AI?

Scams cost older people more than ever. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center logged 201,266 complaints from people aged 60 and over in 2025, with losses of about $7.7 billion, up 59% on 2024. Complaints that mentioned AI cost over-60s about $352 million. These are US figures, but the same tricks travel.

AI makes old scams more convincing. The FBI says criminals use it to write fluent messages without spelling mistakes, create fake profile photos for romance and investment scams, produce fake promotional videos for investment schemes, and clone the voices of relatives. Investment fraud was the costliest scam for over-60s in the FBI's 2025 report, followed by fake tech-support and customer-service calls. Cloned voices of family members are the scam most families worry about; our guide to [AI voice clone scams](/live-with-it/ai-voice-clone-scams) explains how they work and how to protect yourself.

A few rules beat almost all of them:

- **A voice, face or message is not proof.** If someone asks for money or personal details, hang up and call back on a number you already know.
- **Be suspicious of famous faces selling investments.** A video of a celebrity or politician promising easy returns may be a [deepfake](/glossary/deepfake).
- **Never pay with gift cards, cryptocurrency or a cash courier.** The FBI says not to send money, gift cards or cryptocurrency to people you know only from online or the phone.
- **Agree a family safe word** that a real relative can give in an emergency.

## How do I get started?

Go slowly. Age UK's advice is to "go at a pace that feels right for you."

1. **Start with what you already own.** Look in your phone's settings under Accessibility for captions, text size and Magnifier. These are free, and some, such as Android's Live Caption, run entirely on the phone.
2. **Pick one small job.** Ask a chatbot to explain a letter (with names and account numbers covered or removed), suggest a recipe from what is in your fridge, or plan a day out. One useful success is worth more than ten features.
3. **Use the official app.** Download chatbots only from your phone's official app store, and check the maker's name. Many have a free version; you don't need to pay to try one. Our comparison of [AI assistants](/use/chatbots-compared) explains the main ones.
4. **Talk to it like a person.** Plain sentences work. Say what you want, and if the answer is too long or too technical, say "shorter, please" or "explain that more simply".
5. **Ask for help.** Age UK suggests asking a tech-savvy friend or relative, or your local Age UK, about digital support.

## How can families help?

Set things up together rather than doing it for them. Sit side by side, switch on captions or a larger text size, and try one task the person actually cares about, such as reading a recipe aloud or writing to a grandchild.

Then talk about scams calmly and without blame. Agree the safe word over a meal, add the bank's real phone number to their contacts, and make it normal to say "I'll call you back" to any urgent request. The FTC says older people's reported losses to fraud rose about fourfold between 2020 and 2024, and that the typical reported loss for people aged 80 and over was more than $1,600. A short, kind conversation now is the cheapest protection a family has.
