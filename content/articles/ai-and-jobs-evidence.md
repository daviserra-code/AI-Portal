The best data so far points the same way: AI has not caused mass unemployment, but it is changing who gets hired. The effect shows up first among young people starting out in office and software jobs, and mostly as fewer openings rather than layoffs.

This page looks at the evidence itself: what the studies measured, what they found and where they disagree. For how AI is changing day-to-day work, and your rights in the EU, see our companion piece on [AI at work](/live-with-it/ai-at-work).

## Exposure, automation, augmentation: why the words matter

Almost every headline about AI and jobs rests on one of three ideas, and they are easy to mix up.

- **Exposure** means a [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai) tool *could* do some of the tasks in a job. It says nothing about whether anyone actually uses it, or whether the job goes away.
- **Automation** means the AI does a task largely on its own, with little human involvement, so less human work is needed for it.
- **Augmentation** means a person uses AI to do their own work faster or better: drafting, checking, learning, brainstorming. The person stays in charge.

The gap between "could" and "does" is large. In a March 2026 study, Anthropic's economists compared what [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model) could in theory do with how its own [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot), Claude, is actually used at work. In computer and maths jobs, 94% of tasks were rated feasible for AI, but only 33% showed up in real use.

The International Labour Organization (ILO), the UN agency for work, says much the same in its 2025 update. It expects "most jobs will be transformed rather than made redundant," because human input remains necessary. Its researchers describe the bundle of tasks that makes up a job as "a buffer against full automation," while warning that efficiency gains could still mean fewer workers are needed.

The split between automation and augmentation turns out to matter most. Anthropic's usage data for November 2025, published in January 2026, found that 52% of conversations on its consumer app were augmentation and 45% automation. When businesses plug the AI directly into their own systems, the picture flips: about three-quarters of those interactions are automation.

## What has happened to jobs overall?

Across the economy as a whole, researchers keep finding very little.

- **The US.** The Budget Lab at Yale, using government survey data up to the first quarter of 2026, found the employment gap between AI-exposed and less-exposed jobs was "virtually zero," with no significant effect on wages. Unemployment in exposed jobs had ticked up slightly, but not by enough to rule out chance.
- **A second US check.** Anthropic's March 2026 study also found no systematic rise in unemployment for highly exposed workers since late 2022.
- **The euro area.** In a March 2026 blog post, two European Central Bank (ECB) economists looked at a survey of about 5,300 firms. Two-thirds said their staff use AI, but there was no significant difference in hiring or firing between AI users and non-users. Firms that use AI heavily were about 4% more likely to add staff. The authors conclude that AI is not yet replacing jobs in Europe.
- **The OECD.** In August 2026 Stéphane Carcillo, who heads its jobs and income division, wrote that young people across OECD countries are finding it harder to enter work, but that current evidence does not show AI is driving this. The trend began before chatbots became widespread.

All of these are snapshots. The ECB authors stress their data shows correlation, not cause, and that the longer-term impact "remains less clear."

## So where is AI showing up?

In hiring of young people, in specific jobs. The Stanford Digital Economy Lab, using payroll records from the firm ADP, published its latest figures on 12 August 2026. For 22-to-25-year-olds in the most AI-exposed occupations, employment was about 19% below where it would have been had it kept pace with less exposed jobs.

Three details in that study are worth knowing:

- **It is hiring, not firing.** The gap comes mainly from fewer people being taken on, not from existing staff being let go.
- **Automation is the problem, not AI as such.** The declines are concentrated in jobs where AI tends to do tasks on its own. Where AI mostly helps people, employment is flat or rising, especially for experienced workers.
- **Know-how protects you.** Jobs built on knowledge you only learn by doing, through practice and mentoring, held up better for experienced staff. Jobs built on knowledge you can learn from a book or manual fell for young workers.

Anthropic's study points the same way from different data. Unemployment among young exposed workers was flat, but the rate at which young people found jobs in exposed occupations fell about 14% compared with 2022. The authors call that barely statistically significant.

Both teams say plainly that their results are descriptive, not proof that AI is the cause. Stanford notes that some of the trends began before generative AI, and that its gaps shrink once education is taken into account.

## Which tasks are most and least exposed?

The studies broadly agree on the shape. The most exposed tasks are the ones done at a screen with text, numbers or code: writing and fixing software, data entry, customer service replies, financial analysis, routine office paperwork. In Anthropic's measure, computer programmers topped the list at 75% of tasks covered, with data entry keyers at 67%.

The least exposed involve the physical world, people in the room, or both. About 30% of US workers had no measurable coverage at all in Anthropic's data, including cooks, bartenders and lifeguards.

Exposure also falls on different people than earlier waves of automation did. In high-income countries, the ILO counts 34% of jobs as having some exposure, and women are more exposed than men: 41% of employed women compared with 28% of men. In Europe and Central Asia the figures are 39% for women and 26% for men. Anthropic found exposed US workers earn about 47% more on average than unexposed ones.

## Are companies cutting jobs because of AI?

Some say so openly. In June 2025 Amazon's chief executive, Andy Jassy, told staff that "we will need fewer people doing some of the jobs that are being done today" as AI spreads. In September 2025 Salesforce's chief executive, Marc Benioff, said on a podcast that the company had cut its support staff from about 9,000 to about 5,000 because AI [agents](/glossary/ai-agent) now handle many conversations, ITPro reported.

The US outplacement firm Challenger, Gray & Christmas tracks the reasons employers give for announced cuts. In its report of 5 November 2026, AI was cited for 121,401 US cuts in 2026 so far, about 20% of the total. That made it the second most common reason, after market and economic conditions. October itself had only 1,265 AI-related cuts, the lowest monthly total this year.

Two cautions apply. Announced cuts are not the same as jobs lost, and the stated reason is whatever the company chooses to say. Earlier in the year Andy Challenger, of the same firm, put it this way: "while AI is shifting the labor market, it is not dismantling it."

## What about Europe?

European data is thinner than American, partly because AI adoption is newer. According to Eurostat, 20% of EU businesses with 10 or more employees used AI in 2025, up from 13.5% a year earlier. Use ranged from 42% in Denmark to 5.2% in Romania.

The ECB findings above are the best direct European evidence on jobs, and they are reassuring for now. The OECD's Employment Outlook 2026, published in July, adds a warning for later: it lists AI among the shocks reshaping local job markets, and notes that workers displaced by such shocks often carry "lasting scars."

## What is still uncertain?

A lot, and the researchers say so.

- **Whether AI is the cause.** Other things moved at the same time, such as the rise of remote work, which the Yale team flags, and hiring trends that began before chatbots, as the OECD and Stanford note. None of the studies above claims to have fully separated them.
- **Whether exposure is measured right.** Most measures are built from lists of job tasks and from what AI could do in 2023 or from one company's usage data. If some exposed jobs gain while others lose, averages can hide both.
- **What happens as the tools improve.** Anthropic's usage data shows businesses moving towards automated use, especially for coding. If the Stanford pattern holds, more automation would mean more pressure on entry-level hiring.
- **Who benefits.** The International Monetary Fund (IMF) found in January 2026 that about one in ten job vacancies in advanced economies now asks for at least one new skill, and that jobs asking for AI skills pay more. Its economists warn the gains go mainly to high-skilled workers and could contribute to a shrinking middle class.

## What can I do if I'm worried?

- **Sort your own tasks.** List what you actually do in a week. Mark the tasks an AI could do on its own, the ones it could help with, and the ones that need you. The evidence says the first group is where jobs change, so the more of your week sits in the other two, the better placed you are.
- **Use AI to help, not to hand over.** The jobs holding up best are the ones where AI is used to support people. Getting good at checking and steering AI output, including spotting its [hallucinations](/glossary/hallucination), puts you on that side.
- **Invest in know-how that can't be written down.** Client relationships, judgement built over years, practical skill on site: the Stanford data suggests this kind of experience protected workers in exposed jobs.
- **If you are starting out, expect a tougher door.** Entry-level openings in exposed office and software roles are where the squeeze shows. Internships, apprenticeships and roles with real mentoring are worth more than they used to be.
- **Watch hiring in your field, not headlines.** Job ads, graduate intakes and whether colleagues who leave are replaced tell you more than any national figure.
