A [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) will explain the subjunctive, the offside rule or a spreadsheet formula as many times as you ask, in as many ways as you need, at two in the morning, without sighing. That makes it one of the most patient tutors ever built.

The catch is that the same tool will also just give you the answer. Whether you learn anything depends less on which app you pick and more on how you use it. This guide is for adults and older students learning on their own. If you are a parent or student dealing with school rules, cheating and AI detectors, read [AI at school](/live-with-it/ai-at-school) instead.

## Which chatbots have a study or learning mode?

The three biggest assistants have all added a setting that changes how they behave when you are trying to learn rather than get a job done.

- **ChatGPT: study mode.** OpenAI [launched it](https://openai.com/index/chatgpt-study-mode/) in July 2025 for logged-in users on Free, Plus, Pro and Team plans. It uses questions, hints and quick knowledge checks instead of full answers, and adjusts to your level. OpenAI says it works through special instructions written with teachers and learning scientists, and warns that this can mean "inconsistent behavior and mistakes across conversations."
- **Gemini: Guided Learning.** Google [added it](https://techcrunch.com/2025/08/06/google-takes-on-chatgpts-study-mode-with-new-guided-learning-tool-in-gemini/) in August 2025. It breaks problems into stages and can use diagrams, videos and quizzes. On the web, Google's [help page](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/16448384) says you find it under "More tools" in the message box, and that it is still rolling out gradually in the mobile app.
- **Claude: Learning style.** Anthropic first built it for universities and opened it to all Claude.ai users in August 2025, [Engadget reported](https://engadget.com/ai/anthropic-brings-claudes-learning-mode-to-regular-users-and-devs-170018471.html). You pick "Learning" from the style menu, and Claude guides you towards the answer with questions rather than giving it straight away.

Menus move around often, so if you can't find the option, search the app's help pages for "study" or "learning". And every one of these modes can be switched off with a click, so they only help if you leave them on. For a broader comparison of the assistants themselves, see [which AI assistant should you use](/use/chatbots-compared).

## How do you practise a language with AI?

Speaking is the hardest part of a language to practise alone, and it is where AI helps most. You have three main options.

**A general chatbot in voice mode.** Most of the big assistants now let you talk out loud and hear a spoken reply, though limits on free plans differ; our [chatbot comparison](/use/chatbots-compared) lists them. Tell it your level and what you want before you start: "I'm a beginner in Italian. Let's role-play ordering at a café. Speak slowly, use simple words, and after each of my answers, correct one mistake."

**A language app with AI built in.** Duolingo's paid Max tier adds a Video Call feature with a character called Lily who chats with you, remembers what you talked about last time and gives you a transcript afterwards, according to [Duolingo's own description](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-max). It also offers Roleplay scenarios with feedback in some language pairs. Check which features your language gets before paying, because Duolingo lists fewer for some courses.

**Google Translate's practice mode.** In August 2025 Google started [piloting a practice feature](https://blog.google/products/translate/language-learning-live-translate/) in the Translate app on Android and iPhone. It builds listening and speaking exercises around your level and goals. At launch it covered English speakers learning Spanish and French, and Spanish, French and Portuguese speakers learning English, as a beta.

A few habits make practice more useful:

- Ask for corrections at the end of a turn, not in the middle of every sentence, so the conversation keeps flowing.
- Ask it to keep a running list of your repeated mistakes and to quiz you on them next time.
- Request real situations you will face: a doctor's appointment, a phone call to a landlord, small talk with colleagues.
- Treat its grammar explanations as a starting point. It can be confidently wrong about rare rules and regional usage, so check anything important in a grammar book or with a native speaker.

## Can AI help you learn a hobby or practical skill?

Yes, as long as the skill can be explained in words, pictures or steps. A chatbot can build a plan, explain why something works, and help you figure out what went wrong.

Some ways people use it:

- **Make a learning plan.** "I want to learn basic watercolour in six weeks, 20 minutes a day. Give me a weekly plan with one exercise per day."
- **Explain at your level.** "Explain how a mortgage works as if I'm 15, then explain it again as if I work in a bank."
- **Diagnose mistakes.** Many assistants can look at a photo. Show it your sourdough crumb or your chess position and ask what likely went wrong and what to try next.
- **Practise recall.** Paste in your notes from a course and ask for ten questions, one at a time, without the answers.

Where it falls short is anything physical that needs a trained eye on your body: lifting technique, a golf swing, an instrument. It can tell you what to watch for, but it can't see what a coach sees, and a wrong tip can cause injury.

## Which prompts help you actually learn?

The principle is simple: make the AI do the explaining and checking, and keep the thinking for yourself. Our guide to [getting better answers](/use/how-to-write-a-good-prompt) covers [prompts](/glossary/prompt) in general; these are the ones that suit learning.

- **"Don't give me the answer. Give me a hint."** Then ask for a bigger hint only if you are still stuck.
- **"Quiz me on this, one question at a time, and wait for my answer."** Being made to remember something is one of the best ways to make it stick.
- **"Explain this step by step, and stop after each step to check I follow."**
- **"Here is my attempt. Find the mistake, but don't fix it."**
- **"Ask me questions to find out what I already know before you explain."**
- **"Give me an example, then a counter-example."** Seeing where a rule stops working shows you what it really means.
- **"Explain it to me as a story, an analogy or a diagram."** If one explanation doesn't land, ask for a different kind.

A useful test after any session: could you now do a similar task without the chatbot? If not, it did the learning for you.

## What does the research say about learning with AI?

The evidence is still early, but it points in one direction: AI that thinks for you can leave you worse off, and AI that makes you think can help.

The clearest study so far was published in [PNAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/nas/journl/v122y2025pe2422633122.html) in 2025 by researchers at the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton School. They gave nearly 1,000 secondary school maths students in Turkey either a plain GPT-4 chatbot, a version set up to give hints instead of answers, or no AI. During practice, the plain chatbot group did 48% better than the group without AI, and the hint-only group 127% better, [Knowledge at Wharton reports](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/without-guardrails-generative-ai-can-harm-education). But on the exam, taken without AI, the plain chatbot group scored 17% lower than students who never had it, while the hint-only group did about the same.

Two details stand out. According to [PsyPost's summary](https://www.psypost.org/unrestricted-generative-ai-harms-high-school-math-learning-by-acting-as-a-crutch/), most students with the plain chatbot simply asked it for the answer, and it was right only 51% of the time. And those students believed they had learned as much as everyone else.

Adults aren't immune. In a 2025 Microsoft Research survey of 319 knowledge workers, people who had more confidence in AI reported putting less effort into thinking critically about its output, [The Register reports](https://www.theregister.com/2025/02/11/microsoft_study_ai_critical_thinking/). People more confident in their own skills checked the AI's work more.

A widely shared MIT Media Lab study recorded the brain activity of 54 people writing essays and found the lowest brain connectivity in the group using ChatGPT. It is a small preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, and its lead author has said it found no "brain rot", [Tech & Learning notes](https://www.techlearning.com/news/your-brain-on-chatgpt-everything-educators-need-to-know-about-mits-ai-study). Treat it as a warning sign worth watching, not a verdict.

The companies see the problem too. When Anthropic studied about 575,000 student conversations with Claude, it found that nearly half were "direct", students looking for answers or finished content with little engagement, and it wrote of "legitimate worries" that AI could become "a crutch", according to [its own report](https://www.anthropic.com/research/anthropic-education-report-how-university-students-use-claude). That is partly why the learning modes above exist.

## What should you always double-check?

Chatbots run on [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model), which predict likely words rather than look up facts. They [hallucinate](/glossary/hallucination): they state wrong things fluently and confidently. The maths chatbot in the Wharton study was wrong about half the time. Our explainer on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) shows how to catch it.

When you are learning, you are the person least able to spot an error, so be strict:

- **Facts, dates and numbers:** check them in a textbook, an official site or a trusted reference.
- **Maths and calculations:** work through the steps yourself, or check with a calculator.
- **Sources and quotes:** chatbots can invent book titles, papers and quotations. Look them up before you rely on them.
- **Language rules:** confirm unusual grammar points and idioms with a good dictionary or a native speaker.
- **Health, money, law and safety:** use the chatbot to understand the terms, then get the real answer from a qualified person.

One more thing to think about is what you type in. Notes, essays and photos you upload may be stored or used to train future models; our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) shows where the settings are.
