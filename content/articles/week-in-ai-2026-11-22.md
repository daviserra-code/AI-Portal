This was a week about who is in control: of AI companies, of the machines that run airports and banks, and of the computer on your own desk. Here are the seven stories that matter most.

## Anthropic sells shares to the public in a record debut

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot), started trading on the Nasdaq stock exchange on Thursday 19 November, [CBS News reports](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/anthropic-ipo-nasdaq-debut-claude-ai/). It sold shares at $83 and raised $81.3 billion, which CBS calls the largest initial public offering in history, ahead of SpaceX's $75 billion in June. The shares closed the first day at $97.17, about 17% higher, even as the wider market fell.

CBS describes Anthropic as the first frontier AI lab to go public. The company was valued at about $1.5 trillion at its offer price. It has not yet made a profit, and it has committed to nearly $1 trillion of spending on chips and data centres through 2045.

What it means: nothing changes in how Claude works. But a share sale means Anthropic now answers to public shareholders as well as its own safety promises, and CBS notes that people with a US 401(k) or other stock market savings may already be exposed to AI companies whether they buy shares or not. One disclosure from us: Anthropic makes the AI that helps draft this site.

## A cabinet secretary blames AI for outages, without evidence

A run of failures hit the US last week. Microsoft's email and Teams went down worldwide, the FAA grounded flights nationwide for only the second time since 9/11, and JPMorgan Chase's online banking was disrupted, [Fortune reports](https://fortune.com/2026/11/17/trump-administration-ai-outages-mullin-gary-marcus-banks-airlines/). Microsoft and Chase both blamed software bugs, and the FAA said it found no sign of a cyberattack.

On Monday 16 November, Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin told Fox News host Sean Hannity: "I believe that it's the SI", using the administration's name for AI, [The Next Web reports](https://thenextweb.com/news/mullin-reportedly-faces-criticism-inside-administration-over-outages). He said the idea came from an unnamed contact in the tech industry. FAA chief Bryan Bedford had already said: "We see zero evidence of anything nefarious, and zero evidence of anything AI-related." According to The Next Web, the FAA has stopped installing a type of new router linked to the ground stop, and has not yet found the root cause.

What it means: so far, no company or agency involved has said AI caused any of these failures. Treat confident claims about hidden AI causes, from anyone, as a guess until someone shows the evidence. Our page on [SI, AI and AGI](/super-intelligence/si-ai-or-agi) explains why the US government now says "SI".

## Google's Gemini can now use your Mac for you

Google announced on 19 November that its Gemini app for Mac can now carry out tasks on the computer itself, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/google-gives-gemini-the-ability-to-use-your-mac-for-you/). It can open apps, browse websites in the background, fill in forms, sort files into folders and build slideshows. It is available first in English in the US, to people who pay for Google AI Plus, Pro or Ultra. Google has not said when it reaches other countries.

You watch Gemini work in a live preview, and you can pause it, stop it and approve its actions before they happen. TechCrunch notes that OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta already offer similar features in their desktop apps.

What it means: [AI agents](/glossary/ai-agent), tools that act for you rather than just answer, are moving onto ordinary laptops. If you try one, keep the approval step switched on, and start with low-stakes jobs like tidying files rather than anything involving payments. Our explainer on [AI agents on your computer](/understand/ai-agents-on-your-computer) covers what they can see and do.

## Meta's Muse assistant reportedly mixed up people's private data

A Reuters investigation based on Meta's internal posts found that Muse, Meta's AI agent, mixed up conversations between accounts during employee testing, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/19/metas-ai-agent-muse-is-mixing-up-conversations-and-exposing-private-data-report-says/). Employees saw childcare requests and product orders they had never made, and in one case a chat about the weather included someone else's bank account balances. Another employee said Muse reminded her to answer her sister's message on Signal, although she had not given it access to other apps.

A Meta spokesperson said "employee testing is core to the product development process" and that employees use different versions of Muse than the public. TechCrunch says Muse launched in September and has more than 10 million downloads. The report does not say whether the problems reached public users or have been fixed.

What it means: an assistant that can read your messages and accounts can also leak them. Before you connect an AI app to your email, bank or chats, check which permissions it asks for and switch off the ones it doesn't need. Our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) explains where your conversations go.

## Canva adds a switch to turn its AI off

Canva, the design app, now lets free and paid users turn off its [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai) tools, [PetaPixel reports](https://petapixel.com/2026/11/19/canva-now-lets-users-turn-off-generative-ai-features/). The setting, "Enable generative AI features" under Profile, Settings, Permissions, disables tools such as Magic Write, Magic Expand, Magic Design and Dream Lab, and stops image generation in Canva's chatbot. Background Remover and Magic Grab stay on, because Canva says they "make everyday tasks faster without generating anything new."

A second setting, under AI Personalization, shows less AI-made content in search, putting human-made templates and graphics first. Canva says only a "small portion" of its library is AI-generated.

What it means: if you, your school or your small business would rather not use AI-made images or text, you now have a clear way to say so in one of the most popular design tools. It is worth checking other apps you use for a similar setting.

## Students sue after an AI gun detector flagged a bag of Doritos

Four Black teenagers sued in Baltimore County Circuit Court in Maryland on Tuesday 17 November, [WBZ NewsRadio reports](https://wbznewsradio.iheart.com/content/2026-11-18-high-school-students-sue-over-ai-system-that-flagged-doritos-bag-as-gun/). In October 2025, outside Kenwood High School, a camera system called Omnilert Gun Detect flagged one of them as possibly holding a gun. He was holding a bag of Doritos. According to [International Business Times](https://www.yahoo.com/news/us/articles/ai-flagged-doritos-bag-gun-144331761.html), school safety officials and a police representative cancelled the alert within two minutes, which Maryland's inspector general confirmed.

The lawsuit claims the alert was still passed to police, and that officers in at least six cars handcuffed and searched the students. No weapon was found. These claims have not been tested in court. The students are seeking damages and changes to how schools and police handle AI alerts. Omnilert told CBS News Baltimore its system worked as designed, IBTimes reports, and WBZ says the state's inspector general found no evidence of racial bias in the software.

What it means: an AI alert is a guess, and what matters is what people do with it next. If your child's school uses AI cameras, it is fair to ask how a false alarm is checked before anyone calls the police. Our guide to [AI at school](/live-with-it/ai-at-school) has more questions worth asking.

## Trump and Xi split over global AI rules

At the APEC summit of Asia-Pacific leaders in Shenzhen, China, President Trump said the US opposes "any form of globalist regulation or anything to control and stifle SI", [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/11/19/us-president-trump-rejects-global-ai-regulation-as-chinas-xi-calls-for-cooperation-on-safe). He also told leaders the US is "proud to be the world leader in superintelligence", [according to Fox News](https://www.foxnews.com/politics/trumps-china-trip-historic-xi-putin-talks-superintelligence-boast-other-top-moments). China's Xi Jinping told Trump, according to the Chinese news agency Xinhua, that the two countries should cooperate to keep AI "under human control". Canada's Prime Minister Mark Carney said the lack of global AI rules poses an "enormous risk".

Euronews reports no new agreement from these talks, though the two leaders agreed in September to set up a channel for urgent contact about AI incidents, with details still being worked out.

What it means: the world's two biggest AI powers still don't agree on whether AI needs shared international rules, so for now the rules that affect you come from your own country and, in Europe, from the [AI Act](/glossary/ai-act). Our explainer on [who regulates AI](/super-intelligence/who-regulates-ai) shows how these pieces fit together.
