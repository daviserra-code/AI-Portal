A [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) is a decent kitchen assistant for anything that is mostly words and arithmetic: "what can I make with these leftovers", "halve this recipe", "turn the week's dinners into a shopping list". It is a poor judge of whether chicken is cooked, how many calories are on your plate, or whether a dish is safe for someone with a nut allergy.

That split is the whole guide. Let the AI do the planning and the sums. Do the safety checks yourself.

## What is AI actually good at in the kitchen?

Chatbots run on [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model), which are trained on huge amounts of text, including a great many recipes. That makes them quick at the jobs that used to mean flicking through cookbooks:

- **Dinner from what you have.** List what is in the fridge and cupboard, how long you have and what equipment you have, and ask for two or three ideas. Our guide to [writing a good prompt](/use/how-to-write-a-good-prompt) has a worked example of exactly this.
- **A week of meals.** Ask for five dinners that share ingredients, so half a cabbage on Monday gets used up by Thursday. You can add a budget, a time limit or "no fish".
- **Adapting a recipe.** Making a dish vegetarian, swapping an ingredient you can't find in your country, or cutting the steps down for a weeknight.
- **Scaling and converting.** Turning a recipe for four into one for six, or converting cups and ounces from an American recipe into grams and millilitres. Check the sums on anything that matters, such as yeast, salt or baking powder, because small errors there ruin a bake.
- **Shopping lists.** Paste in the week's recipes and ask for one list, grouped by supermarket aisle, with quantities added up.
- **A photo of the fridge.** Many chatbots are now [multimodal](/glossary/multimodal), meaning they can look at pictures as well as read text. A photo of your shelves or a market haul is a fast way to start, though the chatbot can misread what it sees, so correct its list before you cook from it.

## Where do AI recipes go wrong?

The core problem is [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination): a chatbot writes something that sounds right but isn't, in the same confident tone it uses for true facts. Our explainer on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) covers why it happens. In cooking, it shows up in four ways.

**Recipes nobody has tested.** A chatbot can assemble a recipe that has never been cooked. Adam Gallagher, who runs the recipe site Inspired Taste, told NPR in January 2026 that AI tools stitch together "Frankenstein recipes" from pieces of human-written ones. Searching for his hummus recipe could return an AI version "riddled with errors that's obviously never been tested," he said. "Computers don't, the last time I checked, they don't taste or test."

**Wrong times and temperatures.** Bloomberg, in a report republished by Fortune in November 2025, described the food blogger Eb Gargano, of Easy Peasy Foodie, finding an AI-assembled version of her Christmas cake that would have people bake a 15 cm (6-inch) cake at 160°C for three to four hours. "You'd end up with charcoal!" she said.

**Outright dangerous suggestions.** In 2023 the New Zealand supermarket chain Pak'nSave offered an AI meal planner that built recipes from ingredients users typed in. When people entered household products, it suggested an "aromatic water mix" that would produce chlorine gas, the Irish Examiner reported, and called it "the perfect nonalcoholic beverage." The company said "a small minority" had used the tool inappropriately, and the app warned that its recipes were not reviewed by a person. The case shows what can happen when nobody checks the output.

**Shaky food-safety judgement.** Food scientists at Purdue University tested four chatbots (ChatGPT-3.5, Google Bard, Meta AI and Microsoft Copilot) on 20 everyday food practices, such as cooking with aluminium foil or storing celery and carrots in water, and compared the answers with food-safety specialists. Copilot agreed with the experts 80% of the time, Bard 60%, Meta AI 45% and ChatGPT 40%, according to the university's September 2026 summary of the study in the Journal of Food Protection. The bots also changed their verdicts when a question was reworded and invented journal citations. The versions tested are older than today's chatbots, so treat the exact figures as a warning rather than a ranking. Yaohua Feng, who led the work, put the lesson simply: "you can use AI to begin your search, but you need to verify before you take action."

## Can I trust the calorie and nutrition numbers?

Treat them as rough estimates. A study in the journal Nutrients, published in January 2025, asked Gemini, Microsoft Copilot and ChatGPT 4.0 for weight-loss diet plans between 1,400 and 1,800 kcal. The menus were broadly healthy, but the calorie totals were often off: for Gemini, more than half of the plans missed the requested target by over 20%. ChatGPT was the most precise of the three.

Photos are harder still. A November 2025 study, also in Nutrients, tested ChatGPT-5 on 195 dishes. From a photo alone, its calorie estimates were off by about 31% on average. When the researchers added the ingredients and their amounts, the average error fell to about 14%.

So a chatbot can tell you that a creamy pasta is heavier than a lentil soup. It should not be the basis for a medically prescribed diet, insulin doses or a child's growth plan. If precise numbers matter, weigh the ingredients and use the nutrition labels.

## Why do allergies and medical diets need extra care?

Because that is where small mistakes do the most harm.

**Allergens slip into "safe" menus.** In a 2023 study in the journal Nutrition, researchers P. Niszczota and I. Rybicka asked ChatGPT for 56 diets for a hypothetical person with a food allergy, which a dietitian then checked. Most were accurate, but safety was the weakest point: one nut-free diet included almond milk, News-Medical reports. The authors warned that accepting such results "without proper assessments could pose health risks."

**Allergy answers that sound expert but aren't.** A team of German allergists, Mathes and colleagues, writing in March 2025 in The Journal of Allergy and Clinical Immunology: In Practice, put 120 typical allergy questions to ChatGPT. The answers read as very human, but more than half contained errors, and 5% contained critical, potentially dangerous ones, according to the American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology's summary. One example was broadly recommending hypoallergenic formula for children with a confirmed cow's milk allergy, which can trigger a severe reaction in some of them.

**Swaps that hurt people.** In August 2025, doctors described in Annals of Internal Medicine: Clinical Cases a man who wanted to cut table salt from his diet and replaced it with sodium bromide, a chemical once used in sedatives, for about three months. He spent three weeks in hospital with bromide poisoning, Live Science reports. His chat history was no longer available, but when the doctors asked ChatGPT what could replace chloride, it also suggested bromide without a health warning.

**The chatbot can't see your packet.** Recipes and factory processes change. The UK Food Standards Agency tells people with allergies: "You should always check the label to ensure it's safe to eat," every time, and to treat "may contain" warnings as a sign of a known risk. When cooking for someone with an allergy, it advises cleaning surfaces and equipment thoroughly, since picking the allergen off a plate is not enough. No chatbot knows what is in the jar in your hand.

In the EU, 14 allergens must be declared on food, the Food Safety Authority of Ireland lists: cereals containing gluten, crustaceans, eggs, fish, peanuts, soybeans, milk, tree nuts, celery, mustard, sesame, sulphites, lupin and molluscs. The European Food Safety Authority's scientific panel, in an opinion published in 2014, found that around 75% of allergic reactions in children are caused by egg, peanut, cow's milk, fish and nuts.

If you or someone you cook for has coeliac disease, diabetes, kidney disease, a serious allergy or any diet set by a doctor, use AI only for ideas and wording, and check anything that touches the diet itself with your doctor or dietitian. Our guide to [AI and health questions](/live-with-it/ai-and-health-questions) explains how to use chatbots safely for medical matters more generally.

## Which food-safety rules should I check myself?

Don't take cooking temperatures or storage times from a chatbot. Your national food agency publishes them, and they are short. The UK's agencies give a good example of what to look for:

- **Cooked through.** The Food Standards Agency says the middle of the food should reach 70°C for 2 minutes, or an equivalent such as 75°C for 30 seconds. Food Standards Scotland says a thermometer in the thickest part should read 75°C or above.
- **Which foods.** Poultry, pork, minced meat, burgers, sausages and kebabs should be steaming hot all the way through, with no pink meat and clear juices, according to Food Standards Scotland. Whole steaks and joints of beef or lamb can be served rare if the outside is properly cooked, but rolled joints cannot.
- **Leftovers.** The FSA says to cool leftovers and put them in the fridge within two hours, eat them within 48 hours or freeze them, and reheat them only once, until steaming hot throughout. Microwaved food can be hot at the edges and cold in the centre, so stir it.
- **Rice.** Food Standards Scotland says not to keep leftover rice for more than 24 hours, and to reheat it only once.

Other European countries' food agencies give similar advice, sometimes with different figures. If a recipe from a chatbot disagrees with your agency, the agency wins.

## What prompts work better?

A good [prompt](/glossary/prompt) gives the chatbot the facts it would otherwise guess. A few patterns that help:

- "I have [list]. I have 30 minutes, a hob and no oven. Suggest two dinners for two adults. Use metric units and Celsius."
- "Scale this recipe from 4 to 6 servings and show your sums for every ingredient." Seeing the arithmetic makes errors easy to spot.
- "Plan five dinners for a family of four that share ingredients, then give me one shopping list grouped by aisle, with total quantities."
- "This recipe is from a US website. Convert it to grams, millilitres and Celsius, and flag anything you're unsure about."
- "Which of the EU's 14 declared allergens could be in this recipe, including in stock, sauces and bread?" This is a useful checklist, not a guarantee.

## What does a quick checklist look like?

Before you cook from an AI recipe:

1. Read it through once like a sceptic. Does the time, temperature and quantity look like a recipe you would find in a cookbook?
2. Check cooking temperatures and storage times against your national food agency.
3. Redo any scaling or conversion for salt, yeast, baking powder and spices.
4. For allergies, read every label yourself, every time, including "may contain" warnings.
5. Keep medical diets with your doctor or dietitian. Use the chatbot for ideas, not rules.
6. Treat calorie counts as estimates, especially from a photo.
7. Never eat or drink anything a chatbot suggests that isn't ordinary food.
