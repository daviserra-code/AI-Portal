The short version: the car you can buy today helps you drive, it does not drive for you. Even the most advanced systems on sale in Europe either need you watching the road the whole time or work only on motorways in slow traffic. Cars with nobody at the wheel do exist, but as taxis run by companies in a limited set of cities, mostly in the US and China.

Car "AI" is mostly cameras and radar read by software trained with [machine learning](/glossary/machine-learning), plus, lately, [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot)-style voice assistants.

## What do the "levels" of self-driving mean?

Engineers use a scale from the SAE, a standards body for the car industry, called J3016. It runs from 0 to 5, and the key question at each level is simple: who is responsible for watching the road? According to the [ANSI summary of the standard](https://blog.ansi.org/sae-levels-driving-automation-j-3016-2021/):

- **Level 0:** you do all the driving, though warnings may beep at you.
- **Level 1:** the car handles either steering or speed, not both. Adaptive cruise control is the classic example.
- **Level 2:** the car steers and controls speed at the same time, but you must spot hazards and supervise it constantly. This is where almost every "autopilot" style system on sale sits.
- **Level 3:** the car does all the driving in specific conditions, but you must be ready to take over when it asks.
- **Level 4:** the car drives itself within a defined area or set of conditions and will not expect you to step in. Robotaxis are Level 4.
- **Level 5:** the car drives itself anywhere, in any conditions. Nobody sells one.

The big jump is between 2 and 3. Below it, you are the driver even with your hands off the wheel.

## What do the assistance features in my car actually do?

The US road safety regulator, NHTSA, has a [plain guide](https://www.nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/driver-assistance-technologies) to the common ones:

- **Automatic emergency braking** brakes for you to avoid or soften a crash with the vehicle ahead.
- **Lane departure warning** uses a camera to watch the lane markings and alerts you if you drift. It does not steer.
- **Lane keeping assistance** nudges the car back into its lane if it starts to drift.
- **Lane centering** steers continuously to keep you in the middle of the lane.
- **Adaptive cruise control** keeps a set distance from the car in front by speeding up and slowing down.
- **Blind spot warning** alerts you to vehicles you may not see in the next lane.

NHTSA is clear that with features at Levels 0 to 2, the driver must stay fully engaged and stays responsible.

In the EU, several of these are no longer optional. Since 7 July 2024, every new car sold must have intelligent speed assistance (which warns you when you exceed the limit), reversing detection, drowsiness and attention warnings, and an emergency stop signal, according to the [European Commission](https://single-market-economy.ec.europa.eu/news/mandatory-drivers-assistance-systems-expected-help-save-over-25000-lives-2038-2024-07-05_en). New cars and vans also need lane keeping, automated emergency braking and an event data recorder, a kind of black box that logs what happened around a crash. The Commission expects these rules to help save over 25,000 lives and avoid at least 140,000 serious injuries by 2038.

## Can I buy a car that drives itself?

Not in the full sense. The closest thing in Europe is a Level 3 system that works only in narrow conditions.

Mercedes-Benz's Drive Pilot, an option on the S-Class and EQS, was approved by Germany's Federal Motor Transport Authority in December 2024 to drive itself at up to 95 km/h in flowing traffic on German motorways, [the company says](https://group.mercedes-benz.com/technology/autonomous-driving/driving/drive-pilot-95-kmh.html). While it is active, Mercedes says the driver may do other things, such as watch a film or work. If the driver does not take back control when asked, the car stops itself with the hazard lights on. International rules under UN Regulation 157 were amended to allow such systems up to 130 km/h, with lane changes, from January 2023, [Future Transport-News reported](https://futuretransport-news.com/un-regulation-increases-automated-driving-speed-limit-to-130km-h).

Tesla's "Full Self-Driving (Supervised)" is a Level 2 system despite the name. The Dutch vehicle authority RDW approved it on 10 April 2026, the first approval in Europe, and stressed in [its announcement](https://www.rdw.nl/en/news/2026/rdw-explanation-of-european-type-approval-tesla-with-provisional-validity-in-the-netherlands): "A vehicle with FSD Supervised is not self-driving." The driver "must always remain in control"; sensors check that your eyes are on the road, and reading a newspaper is not allowed. RDW [later reported](https://www.rdw.nl/en/news/2026/explanation-of-the-type-approval-of-fsd-supervised) that nearly 40,000 Teslas had driven about 24 million kilometres with it in the Netherlands "without any relevant incidents."

Other EU countries can accept the Dutch approval on their own. Slovenia did so in September 2026, [The Next Web reported](https://thenextweb.com/news/slovenia-tesla-fsd-approval-eu-vote), and by mid-November eleven European countries were on the list, according to a [tracker by Tesla Mag](https://www.tesla-mag.com/en/?p=89173); France, Germany, Italy and Spain were not. In Europe the feature has been renamed "Tesla Assisted Driving". An EU-wide decision by member states is still pending: Germany's transport ministry said a vote was planned for December 2026, [Not a Tesla App reported](https://www.notateslaapp.com/news/4749/germany-says-it-is-open-to-tesla-fsd-approval-confirms-eu-wide-vote-in-december).

## Where can I ride in a car with no driver?

**United States.** Waymo, owned by Google's parent company Alphabet, is the largest operator. Its service covers Phoenix, the San Francisco Bay Area, Los Angeles, Austin, Atlanta, Miami, Orlando, Dallas, Houston, San Antonio and Nashville, [according to 9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2026/04/22/waymo-new-cities-features/), with some cities still invite-only or booked through Uber. Las Vegas went driverless in July 2026, [Electrek reported](https://electrek.co/2026/07/08/waymo-driverless-las-vegas-four-new-cities/), and since 14 October anyone in San Antonio, San Diego and Tampa can hail a ride, [Waymo says](https://www.waymo.com/blog). Weather still matters: Waymo paused its Bay Area service on 23 November because of severe weather forecasts.

Tesla launched its own Robotaxi service in Austin in June 2025 with a safety monitor in the passenger seat, and began some rides without one in January 2026, though Electrek noted chase cars with staff followed them. By November, Tesla said it ran rides without a safety driver in eight cities in Texas and Florida, [GCN reported](https://gcn.com/?p=23199), but it has not disclosed fleet sizes.

**China and the Middle East.** Baidu's Apollo Go operates in 30 cities, with fully driverless rides in places such as Shenzhen and fully driverless commercial service in Dubai, [Baidu said](https://finviz.com/news/422901/baidu-announces-third-quarter-2026-results) on 16 November.

**Europe.** No public driverless taxi service runs yet. Volkswagen's MOIA started carrying selected, pre-registered residents in self-driving ID. Buzz vans in Hamburg in July 2026, but with a trained safety operator on every ride, [MOIA says](https://www.moia.io/en/news/moia-launches-passenger-test-operation-in-hamburg). Baidu is testing on open roads in London with Uber and Lyft. Waymo said in October 2025 that it would offer a commercial robotaxi service in London in 2026, depending on UK government approval, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/15/waymo-plans-to-launch-a-robotaxi-service-in-london-in-2026); its blog posts up to late November do not announce one. The EU has had type-approval rules for the automated driving systems of fully automated vehicles since 2022, [Regulation 2022/1426](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2022/1426/oj/eng), so the legal route exists.

## Are self-driving cars safer than people?

For robotaxis, the early evidence is encouraging, with caveats. Waymo's own [safety data](https://waymo.com/safety/impact), covering 271 million miles without a driver up to June 2026, claims 95% fewer crashes causing serious injury or worse and 82% fewer injury crashes than human drivers on the same roads. Waymo publishes these figures itself and says they cover city streets, not snow or much motorway driving.

An independent study by the US Insurance Institute for Highway Safety, [published in July 2026](https://iihs.org/news/detail/waymos-driverless-cars-crash-less-often-than-people), found Waymo vehicles were involved in 68% fewer crashes per mile than human drivers, with 81% fewer injury crashes. The institute's president called the cars safer than human drivers but warned that "the present data collection system isn't good enough to allow continuous monitoring of a large-scale expansion."

They are not flawless. In late 2025 NHTSA expanded an investigation after Waymo cars illegally passed stopped school buses; the Austin school district alone counted 20 citations by 1 December, [CBS News reported](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/waymo-investigation-nhtsa-robotaxis-passing-school-bus). Waymo issued a software recall, and on 6 November 2026 NHTSA opened a review of whether that fix worked, covering 2,895 vehicles, [according to Stock Analysis](https://stockanalysis.com/news/waymo-school-bus-recall-probe/).

For Level 2 systems in private cars, the picture is weaker. When the IIHS first rated how well 14 such systems keep drivers paying attention, [in March 2024](https://www.iihs.org/news/detail/first-partial-driving-automation-safeguard-ratings-show-industry-has-work-to-do), only one scored acceptable and eleven were rated poor. Its president said "there is little evidence it makes driving safer." In March 2026, NHTSA escalated its probe into Tesla's Full Self-Driving software over nine crashes in fog, glare and dust, in which the software failed to warn drivers to take over in time; the review could lead to a recall covering 3.2 million vehicles, [AP reported](https://www.boston.com/cars/business/2026/03/19/tesla-faces-wider-probe-of-self-driving-feature-as-it-prepares-to-sell-cars-without-steering-wheels/).

The practical lesson: a system that drives well most of the time can lull you into looking away. If your car says you are the driver, you are.

## Are car voice assistants now chatbots?

Increasingly, yes. The old voice systems understood fixed commands. The new ones pass anything they cannot handle to a [large language model](/glossary/large-language-model), the same technology behind ChatGPT.

Volkswagen began adding ChatGPT to its IDA voice assistant in 2024 in models including the Golf, Tiguan, Passat and ID. range. It says "ChatGPT does not gain access to any vehicle data; questions and answers are deleted immediately," according to [Volkswagen Group](https://www.volkswagen-group.com/en/articles/world-premiere-at-ces-volkswagen-integrates-chatgpt-into-its-vehicles-18052). In April 2026 Google began replacing Google Assistant with Gemini in cars that have Google built-in, starting in English in the US. [Google says](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/platforms/android/cars-with-google-built-in-gemini-tips-2026/) it can find stops, summarise and reply to messages, answer questions from your owner's manual and change settings such as the heating. GM is adding a Gemini-based assistant too, with drivers able to opt in or out, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2025/10/22/gm-is-bringing-google-gemini-powered-ai-assistant-to-cars-in-2026).

These assistants can still [make things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up), so check anything important, such as what a warning light means, against the manual.

## What does my car know about me?

More than most people expect. When the Mozilla Foundation reviewed the privacy policies of 25 car brands in 2023, all 25 failed. It found every brand collected more personal data than it needed, 84% said they could share it, 76% said they could sell it, and 92% gave drivers little or no control, according to [Mozilla's report](https://www.mozillafoundation.org/en/privacynotincluded/articles/its-official-cars-are-the-worst-product-category-we-have-ever-reviewed-for-privacy/). Only Renault and Dacia said all drivers could have their data deleted.

This is not only theory. In January 2026 the US Federal Trade Commission finalised an order against General Motors, which it alleged had collected precise location and driving behaviour data from millions of vehicles through OnStar and sold it to third parties without consent. The [FTC order](https://ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/01/ftc-finalizes-order-settling-allegations-gm-onstar-collected-sold-geolocation-data-without-consumers) bans GM from sharing that data with consumer reporting agencies for five years and requires consent before collecting it.

In the EU you have more tools. The GDPR lets you ask a carmaker for a copy of your personal data or ask for it to be deleted, as with any company; our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) explains how those requests work. Since 12 September 2025, the EU Data Act has also given owners of connected products, including cars, the right to access, use and share the raw data they generate, the [European Commission says](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/eu-data-act-gives-users-control-over-data-connected-devices), which can help you choose an independent repair shop.

It is worth checking the privacy settings on the car's screen and in the maker's app, and, before selling a car or returning a rental, deleting your paired phone and saved addresses from it.
