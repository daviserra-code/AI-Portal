A [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) is a decent money helper for anything that is about your own numbers and plain-language explanations. It is a poor one for anything that depends on today's rates, today's prices or the tax rules of the country you live in. Most of the trouble people get into comes from mixing those two up.

That matters because plenty of people already ask. In a September 2025 survey of 4,189 UK adults for the consumer group Which?, about one in six said they always or often rely on AI for financial advice.

## What is a chatbot actually good at with money?

The jobs it does well have one thing in common: you supply the facts, and it organises or explains them.

- **Building a budget.** Give it your rough monthly income and your regular costs, in round numbers, and ask for a simple monthly plan with a buffer for irregular bills such as car insurance or school trips. Ask it to show the totals so you can check the sums yourself.
- **Explaining a bill or contract term.** Paste the one confusing line, not the whole document, and ask what it means. "What is a standing charge?", "What does an early termination fee usually cover?" and "What is the difference between a fixed and a variable rate?" are the kind of questions it handles well.
- **Comparing options you already have.** If you have two loan offers or two phone contracts in front of you, type in the key terms (price, length, fees) and ask it to set out the differences and the total cost over the full period. It is good at spotting the question you forgot to ask, such as what happens after an introductory price ends.
- **Drafting the awkward message.** A polite email asking a provider to explain a charge, a request to cancel, or a note asking for a payment plan. Our guide to [everyday writing with AI](/use/ai-for-everyday-writing) covers this in more detail.
- **Preparing for a real conversation.** Before you see a bank, a tax office or an adviser, ask it to list the questions worth asking and the documents to bring.

## Where does it go wrong?

### Old rates and prices

A [large language model](/glossary/large-language-model) learns from a snapshot of text, and even chatbots that search the web often pull from pages that are out of date. Which? tested the free version of ChatGPT on UK savings in March 2026 and compared its answers with market data from the same day. It got most average and top rates wrong, quoted one bank's rate as 4.5% when the real figure was 3.75%, missed that another bank's headline rate was a temporary bonus, and gave the old £85,000 limit for the UK's savings protection scheme, which had risen to £120,000 on 1 December 2025.

The lesson travels well beyond the UK: any rate, fee, price or limit a chatbot gives you is a starting point to check with the provider, never a fact to act on.

### Numbers that sound right but aren't

Chatbots sometimes invent figures, a habit called [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination). They also tend to accept the premise of your question. In Which?'s November 2025 test of six AI tools, one money question deliberately overstated the UK's yearly tax-free savings allowance as £25,000 instead of £20,000. ChatGPT and Microsoft Copilot did not spot the error and gave advice that, Which? said, could have led someone to break the tax rules. Gemini, Meta AI and Perplexity corrected it.

So if your own number is wrong, the chatbot may build a confident plan on top of it. Ask it to check your figures, and do the important sums again yourself. Our explainer on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) explains where this comes from.

### Rules that depend on where you live

Tax, benefits, pensions and consumer rights are set largely at national level. The EU's Your Europe portal puts it plainly for income tax: there is no single EU rule, and "each country has its own definition of tax residence." A chatbot trained mostly on English-language pages will often answer with American or British rules unless you tell it otherwise, and even then it may mix them up.

Always say which country you live in, and treat the answer as a map of what to look up on your tax authority's or government's own website.

### Steering you towards a product

In the same Which? test, ChatGPT and Perplexity pointed people asking about a tax refund towards paid refund companies, which Which? describes as notorious for high fees, when a free official tool exists. A chatbot's suggestion is not a recommendation from someone who is responsible for it.

### Investing

The EU markets regulator ESMA and national supervisors published a factsheet in March 2025 telling investors "do not rely blindly on AI." According to Italy's regulator CONSOB, which republished it, the factsheet warns that public AI tools can give misleading advice, promote easy-money claims, sit outside regulatory oversight and carry privacy risks. ESMA's advice is to treat AI as one source among many and to consult a licensed professional when you need one.

## What should I never paste into a chatbot?

What you type is stored, may be read by human reviewers, and could leak. Google's Gemini privacy page says it directly: "Please don't enter confidential information that you wouldn't want a reviewer to see." Our guide to [your data and AI chatbots](/live-with-it/your-data-and-ai-chatbots) shows how to switch off training and delete history, but the safest data is the data you never typed.

For money questions, keep these out:

- **Card numbers, expiry dates and security codes.** No budgeting question needs them.
- **PINs, passwords, one-time codes and online banking logins.** The EU's three financial supervisors (EBA, EIOPA and ESMA) say in their December 2025 scams factsheet that legitimate institutions never ask for these. A chatbot has no reason to either.
- **Your IBAN together with your name, address or ID details.** On its own an IBAN is something you give to employers and landlords. Combined with identity details it becomes far more useful to a fraudster. Germany's consumer advice centres (Verbraucherzentrale) warned in September 2025 that criminals use stolen IBANs to pay for online purchases by direct debit, and advise giving your IBAN only to reputable firms and checking your statements.
- **Full bank statements or payslips.** They carry your account number, address, employer and a detailed picture of your life. Copy the totals you need into a short list instead.
- **Scans of your ID, passport or tax documents.**

A simple habit helps: replace real details with placeholders. "Rent 900, energy 120, Loan A 250" is all a budget needs.

## How are scammers using AI against my money?

The same tools that help you write emails help criminals write better ones. "AI has made them smarter and harder to spot," the EU supervisors' factsheet says of online scams. It lists the main patterns:

- **Impersonation and [deepfakes](/glossary/deepfake):** calls or videos that sound and look like your bank, a public authority or a relative, often with a spoofed phone number and an urgent reason to move money. Our guide to [AI voice clone scams](/live-with-it/ai-voice-clone-scams) covers the family emergency version.
- **Phishing:** fake bank alerts tailored with details scraped from your social media, leading to a lookalike login page.
- **Fake investments:** adverts with AI-generated celebrity endorsements and "limited time" offers that start with a small deposit and then block withdrawals.

In Ireland, the Central Bank warned in November 2025 that AI is being used to create convincing adverts and profiles impersonating public and business figures, and that scams are moving away from wild promises towards returns only slightly better than the market, which makes them harder to spot, RTÉ reported. Gardaí recorded a 21% rise in investment scams in the three months to October 2025.

The supervisors' advice is short: pause before you act, end the call and ring back on a number you already know, never install remote access apps or share your screen, and check a firm on your national regulator's register before sending money. In the UK, the FCA's Warning List of unauthorised firms has more than 14,000 entries, and the FCA notes that a firm missing from the list may still be a scam. If you have been caught, contact your bank at once, report it to the police, and beware of "recovery" firms offering to get your money back for a fee.

One more trap: don't ask a chatbot whether an investment or a website is legitimate and treat a "yes" as proof. It cannot see the firm's licence. The regulator's register can.

## When should I talk to a human instead?

Use a chatbot to understand the question. Go to a person when the answer will cost you real money or is hard to undo:

- **Debt you are struggling with.** The EU's 2023 consumer credit directive requires member states to make independent debt advice available to people who have, or may have, trouble meeting their payments, with only limited charges, and requires lenders to point those customers to it. Your bank or local consumer organisation can tell you where to find it.
- **Investments, pensions and mortgages.** Use an adviser who is authorised by your national regulator, and check them on its register.
- **Tax questions with real consequences**, such as working across a border, selling property, or running a small business. Your tax authority, or an accountant, knows the rules where you live.
- **A dispute with a provider.** Your national consumer organisation can explain your rights and how to complain.

Banks' own chatbots have the same limits. A June 2023 report by the US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau warned that chatbots in consumer finance can give wrong answers that some customers can't detect, and can trap people in "doom loops" with no way to reach a person. If a bank's chatbot can't solve your problem, ask for a human, by phone or in a branch if you have to.
