Most of the [artificial intelligence](/glossary/artificial-intelligence) in healthcare is not a robot doctor. It is software working in the background: a tool that listens to your appointment and writes the notes, a program that marks a suspicious spot on a scan, a system that drafts a reply to your message or flags an emergency call as urgent. You may already have met one without knowing.

This guide is about the AI your doctor or hospital uses. If you want to know about asking a chatbot health questions yourself, see our separate guide, [Can you trust AI with health questions?](/live-with-it/ai-and-health-questions)

## What is an AI scribe, and what does it do in my appointment?

An AI scribe, sometimes called "ambient voice technology", records the conversation between you and your doctor. Speech recognition turns it into text, and a [large language model](/glossary/large-language-model), the same kind of technology behind chatbots, turns that text into a tidy clinical note or a letter. The doctor then reads it, corrects it and saves it to your record.

The promise is simple: less typing, more eye contact. In England, NHS England published a register of 19 approved scribe suppliers in January 2026, [Health Tech World reports](https://new.htworld.co.uk/?p=47347). An evaluation led by Great Ormond Street Hospital across nine London NHS sites, covering more than 17,000 patient encounters, found a 23.5% increase in direct time with patients, appointments 8.2% shorter, and 13.4% more patients seen per shift in A&E.

Those figures come from an evaluation announced by NHS England rather than a randomised trial, the kind of study where patients or doctors are split into groups at random so a fair comparison can be made. The best randomised evidence so far is more modest. A [trial published in NEJM AI](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41497288/) in November 2025 split 238 US outpatient doctors into three groups: two used different scribe products and one kept working as usual. One product cut the time doctors spent on notes by 9.5%; the other made no significant difference. Doctors in both scribe groups reported a lighter workload.

## Can an AI scribe get my notes wrong?

Yes. The same NEJM AI trial found that clinically significant inaccuracies turned up "occasionally" in the notes, and recorded one mild harm to a patient. Language models can [hallucinate](/glossary/hallucination), meaning they write things that sound right but are not, and they can leave things out. Our guide on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) explains why.

NHS England's [guidance on ambient scribes](https://www.england.nhs.uk/long-read/guidance-on-the-use-of-ai-enabled-ambient-scribing-products-in-health-and-care-settings) lists the risks plainly. Products "may struggle with complex clinical terminology, abbreviations, or rapidly spoken dialogue". Worse, "mistaking a patient's hypothetical statement for a confirmed diagnosis" could lead to serious errors in the record. If you say "I was worried it might be my heart", a careless summary could turn that into a heart problem. The guidance also warns about "automation bias": busy staff trusting the output too much.

That is why the rule everywhere is the same: the clinician stays responsible for the note and must check it before it goes into your record.

## Is AI reading my scans?

In some places, yes, mostly as a second pair of eyes rather than a replacement for a radiologist. Breast cancer screening is where the evidence is strongest, and much of it comes from Europe.

In Sweden, the MASAI trial randomly split 106,000 women aged 40 to 74 between normal screening, where two radiologists read every mammogram, and AI-supported screening. The AI gave each mammogram a risk score, sent low-risk ones to a single radiologist and higher-risk ones to two, and marked suspicious areas. According to [Lund University](https://www.lunduniversity.lu.se/article/ai-support-breast-cancer-screening-fewer-missed-cancer-cases), the final results in The Lancet in January 2026 showed:

- 29% more breast cancers found at screening with AI support.
- 82 "interval cancers" (cancers found between screening rounds, often more aggressive) with AI, against 93 without: 12% fewer.
- 44% less reading work for radiologists, with no rise in false alarms.

In Germany, a study of 463,094 women at 12 screening sites, [published in Nature Medicine](https://www.cancernetwork.com/view/ai-supported-mammography-screening-leads-to-increased-breast-cancer-detection-rate) in January 2025, found 6.7 cancers per 1,000 women when radiologists used an AI tool against 5.7 per 1,000 without, with about the same number of women called back for more tests. That study was not randomised: radiologists chose when to use the AI, which can skew results.

For you as a patient, nothing changes in the screening itself. The difference is what happens to your images afterwards.

## Can AI decide how urgent my case is?

AI is being tested for triage, the job of deciding who needs help first. The evidence here is a good reminder that a clever tool does not automatically make care better.

In Copenhagen, researchers built a [machine learning](/glossary/machine-learning) system that listened to emergency calls and alerted the call handler when it suspected a cardiac arrest. In a randomised trial [published in JAMA Network Open](https://busqueda.bvsalud.org/portal/resource/es/mdl-33404620) in 2021, covering 169,049 calls, the AI on its own spotted more cardiac arrests than the human dispatchers (85.0% against 77.5%), but it also raised many more false alarms. When dispatchers got the alerts, they recognised cardiac arrest in 93.1% of cases, against 90.5% without them, a difference the authors found was not significant.

AI is also being used to draft replies to the messages patients send through hospital apps and portals. In a [study in The Lancet Digital Health](https://news.massgeneralbrigham.org/en/pitfalls-and-opportunities-for-generative-ai-in-patient-messaging-systems), six cancer doctors reviewed GPT-4 drafts answering 100 made-up patient questions. They judged 58.3% fine to send without changes. But if sent unedited, 0.6% could have put a patient's life at risk, usually because they failed to tell the patient to seek care urgently, and 7.1% posed some other risk. In 31% of cases the doctors thought the AI draft had been written by a person.

So if a message from your clinic feels oddly vague about something that worries you, phone them.

## Do doctors get worse when they lean on AI?

There is early evidence that they can. A study in four Polish colonoscopy centres, [reported by Healthcare in Europe](https://healthcare-in-europe.com/en/news/ai-support-endoscopy-lose-skills-study.html), followed 19 experienced doctors before and after they started using AI that highlights possible growths in the bowel. When they worked without the AI after getting used to it, their detection rate fell from 28.4% to 22.4%.

The study was observational, so other factors may explain part of the drop. But it raises a fair question for hospitals: what happens on the day the system is down?

## What are my rights?

**Your health data has extra protection.** Under the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), [Article 9](https://gdpr-text.com/en/read/article-9/) treats "data concerning health" as a special category that is generally off limits. Health services may use it for diagnosis and treatment, but only under the law and by people bound by professional secrecy. That is why a hospital does not always need your written consent to use a scribe as part of your care: the legal basis is providing healthcare, not consent.

**You can see and correct your record.** The EU's [Your Europe portal](https://europa.eu/youreurope/citizens/consumers/internet-telecoms/data-protection-online-privacy/index_en.htm) says you can get a free copy of the personal data an organisation holds about you, with a reply due within one month, and ask for mistakes to be corrected. That includes a note an AI helped write. If you are ignored, you can complain to your national data protection authority.

**A machine should not decide alone.** [GDPR Article 22](https://gdpr-text.com/en/read/article-22/) gives you the right "not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing" when it significantly affects you, and adds stricter limits where health data is involved.

**Medical AI counts as high-risk under the AI Act.** The EU's [AI Act](/glossary/ai-act) classes AI built into medical devices as high-risk, which means makers must meet requirements on data quality, risk management, transparency, human oversight and accuracy, according to [law firm Taylor Wessing](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/international-life-sciences-newsletter/life-sciences-legal-lens-vol-2/update-ai-enabled-medical-devices-and-ivds-confirmed-as-high-risk). A change to the law published in July 2026 pushed the start date for these rules on medical devices to 2 August 2028, the [Future of Privacy Forum](https://fpf.org/blog/the-ai-act-implementation-timeline-what-changes-under-the-ai-omnibus) reports. Until then, medical devices still have to meet the EU's existing medical device rules. Separately, since August 2026 the AI Act's [Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/) has required AI systems that talk directly to people, such as a clinic's chatbot, to be built so you are told you are dealing with AI, unless that is obvious. Our [guide to the EU AI Act](/live-with-it/eu-ai-act-explained) covers the law in more depth.

**In the UK, you can say no to a scribe.** The UK left the EU but keeps its own version of GDPR. NHS England's updated guidance from April 2026, [reported by Digital Health](https://www.digitalhealth.net/2026/04/nhse-publishes-fresh-guidance-on-safe-use-of-ambient-scribes/), says clinicians must tell you at the start of the appointment if a scribe is in use, and "if an individual dissents, you cannot then use that tool." Rules in other countries vary, so ask.

## What should I ask my doctor?

You don't need to be an expert to ask good questions. These are reasonable in any appointment:

1. **Is anything recording or listening to this appointment?** If yes, ask who makes the tool and whether you can opt out.
2. **Where does the recording go, and how long is it kept?** Some products delete audio once the note is written; others keep it.
3. **Will you check the note before it goes into my record?** You can also ask for a copy afterwards and point out anything wrong.
4. **Did AI help read my scan or test?** If so, ask whether a specialist also looked at it, and what the AI flagged.
5. **Was this message written by a person?** If an answer from your clinic seems generic or misses your worry, ask to speak to someone.
6. **What happens if I would rather you didn't use it?** You should still get the same care.

A good doctor will not mind being asked. Most of these tools exist to give them more time with you, and a short conversation about how they work is a fair use of some of it.
