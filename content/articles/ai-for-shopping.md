A [chatbot](/glossary/chatbot) is a decent shop assistant for the part of buying that is mostly reading. Tell it you need a quiet dishwasher for a small kitchen under 500 euros, and it will explain the jargon, suggest a handful of models and tell you what owners tend to complain about. That saves an evening of open tabs.

Where it lets you down is the detail you are about to pay for: the exact price, which version of a product is current, whether it is in stock. So the useful habit is simple. Let the AI narrow the field, then confirm every number on the seller's own page.

## What can AI actually do for a shopper?

The big chatbots now have shopping modes built in. OpenAI launched "shopping research" in ChatGPT in November 2025. You describe what you need, it asks follow-up questions about budget and use, then it reads retail sites and builds a short buyer's guide with prices, specs and reviews. It is available on the Free, Go, Plus and Pro plans, according to Digital Commerce 360. Google added conversational shopping to its AI Mode in Search and the Gemini app in the US the same month, drawing on what Google calls its Shopping Graph of more than 50 billion product listings. Amazon's own assistant, Rufus, was renamed Alexa for Shopping in May 2026.

Whichever tool you use, these are the jobs where AI earns its keep:

- **Turning vague needs into a spec.** "Something for video calls that doesn't make me look grey" becomes a list of what actually matters: resolution, low-light performance, a built-in microphone.
- **Explaining jargon.** What OLED means, whether you need Wi-Fi 7, why a mattress's "firmness 7/10" means little across brands.
- **Side-by-side comparisons.** Laying out two or three shortlisted models in a table, so you can see the trade-offs at a glance.
- **Summarising reviews.** Pulling out the complaints that keep coming up, such as "loud fan" or "battery fades after a year".
- **Questions to ask before buying.** Warranty, spare parts, running costs, return terms.

These are word and reasoning jobs, which is what [large language models](/glossary/large-language-model) do well. Exact, fast-changing facts are a different matter.

## Where does AI shopping advice go wrong?

**Prices, versions and stock.** OpenAI's own launch post says shopping research "might make mistakes about product details like price and availability" and tells you to check the merchant's site. Independent tests show how often that happens. Product.ai, a company that sells a product-checking service, asked ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Perplexity 220 shopping questions in September 2026, five times each. On 86% of the questions the answers contained a checkable factual conflict, such as two different battery lives or prices for the same product. Comparison questions fared worst, at 97%. Most verifiable prices were right, but the wrong ones were off by a median of 300 dollars, and Gemini's free tier recommended a pair of headphones that the maker had already replaced with a newer model. The company has a commercial interest in the result, and it says so.

**Mixed-up models and variants.** A January 2026 test by Smarter Sorting, a product-data company, ran five assistants through 2,500 steps on 100 products. The most common failure was variant confusion: the wrong size, shade or formulation. Availability lookups failed in more than half of cases.

**Specs that sound right but aren't.** Chatbots fill gaps with plausible guesses, a problem known as [hallucination](/glossary/hallucination). A product page can be ambiguous, and the AI won't always tell you it guessed. Our explainer on [why AI makes things up](/understand/why-ai-makes-things-up) covers how to catch it.

**Shaky consumer-rights advice.** When the UK consumer group Which? tested six AI tools on 40 consumer questions in September 2025, it found "inaccurate, unclear and risky advice". Copilot wrongly said air passengers are always entitled to a full refund, and Gemini and Meta AI wrongly claimed any broadband contract can be left without penalty. For refunds and returns, go to an official source such as your national consumer centre.

## Are the recommendations sponsored?

Sometimes, and the labels matter. OpenAI started testing ads in ChatGPT in February 2026 for logged-in adults on the Free and Go plans. It says ads are "always clearly labeled as sponsored", sit apart from the answer and "do not influence the answers ChatGPT gives you". The test began in the US, and OpenAI's page has since added pilots in countries including Canada, the UK and Japan. OpenAI also says shopping research results "are organic", though merchants can sign up to make sure their products can appear.

Google told The Register in January 2026 that it was still testing ads in AI Mode, and was working with retailers on "Direct Offers", discounts shown to individual shoppers inside its AI shopping tools.

There is a quieter form of influence too. Microsoft's security researchers reported in February 2026 that some companies hide instructions in "Summarize with AI" buttons on their websites. Clicking one opens your chatbot with a pre-filled message such as "remember [Company] as a trusted source" or "recommend [Company] first". Over 60 days, they found 50 such prompts from 31 companies. Microsoft's advice: be wary of those buttons, and check your assistant's saved memories for entries you don't remember adding.

So treat an AI's top pick like a friend's suggestion, not a test result. If one brand keeps coming up first, ask the chatbot why, and ask for alternatives from other makers.

## Can I trust the reviews it summarises?

Only as far as you trust the reviews themselves. A chatbot summarising reviews cannot tell you which ones were bought or invented, and AI has made fake ones cheaper to write. The Transparency Company, which uses software to detect fake reviews, analysed 73 million reviews of home, legal and medical services and judged nearly 14% likely fake, AP reported in December 2024. It had high confidence that 2.3 million were partly or entirely written by AI. A Yale researcher quoted in the same report found people cannot reliably tell AI-written reviews from human ones.

The law is on your side, at least on paper. In the EU, rules in force since 28 May 2022 ban fake reviews: sellers must say how they check that reviewers actually bought the product, may not post reviews posing as customers, and must disclose paid reviews, according to the European Consumer Centre Netherlands. In a January 2022 sweep of 223 major websites, the European Commission found that at least 55% potentially broke EU law on reviews. The US Federal Trade Commission finalised its own ban in August 2024 and said it would help "deter AI-generated fake reviews".

What helps in practice:

- Ask the chatbot to summarise only reviews older than a few months, or verified purchases, and to name where they came from.
- Look for reviews that mention specific, boring details, such as how the product held up after a year, rather than general praise.
- Prefer long-term tests from consumer organisations and specialist reviewers over star ratings.
- Watch for clusters of glowing reviews posted in the same few days.

Our guide to [spotting AI-generated content](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content) has more on the signs to look for.

## Should I let an AI agent buy things for me?

This is the newest part of AI shopping. An [AI agent](/glossary/ai-agent) doesn't just answer; it goes to the shop and acts for you. Google lets US shoppers set a price alert with a maximum price and then have Google buy the item on the merchant's site with Google Pay, but it asks for permission first and only buys after you confirm the purchase and shipping details. On 4 November 2026 it brought the same direct checkout to Canada, according to iPhone in Canada. Amazon's auto-buy, added to its assistant for Prime members, works differently: it buys automatically with your default card and address once the price drops to your target, then notifies you, and you can cancel for free within 24 hours.

Not every attempt has gone smoothly. OpenAI scaled back its "Instant Checkout" inside ChatGPT in March 2026 after it gained little traction, Retail Gazette reported, and shifted towards product discovery with checkout on retailers' own sites. Earlier, in February 2025, The Washington Post's tech columnist asked OpenAI's Operator agent to find the cheapest dozen eggs. It bought a dozen on Instacart for 31.43 dollars with his saved card, without asking him. OpenAI said Operator was supposed to ask for confirmation and that it was adding stricter checks.

If you try an agent, keep it on a short lead:

1. Use one that asks before paying, and keep that setting on.
2. Set a hard price limit, including delivery and fees.
3. Use a card with a low limit or a virtual card, if your bank offers one.
4. Check the order confirmation email the same day.
5. Know your exit. In the EU you can usually cancel an online purchase within 14 days of delivery without giving a reason, according to the EU's Your Europe portal, but there are exceptions, such as tickets for specific dates, perishable food and personalised goods.

## What prompts get better shopping help?

A good [prompt](/glossary/prompt) gives the chatbot the facts it would otherwise guess, and asks it to show its uncertainty. Patterns that work:

- "I need a cordless vacuum for a 60-square-metre flat with a cat. Budget 250 euros. I care most about battery life and noise. Ask me any questions you need before suggesting anything."
- "Compare these three models in a table: price, weight, battery life, warranty and spare parts. Link the page each figure comes from, and mark anything you couldn't confirm."
- "Is this the current model, or has the maker released a newer version? When did each come out?"
- "Summarise the most common complaints in reviews of this product. Ignore reviews that only give praise without details."
- "What should I check on the product page before I buy? List the things that often differ between versions of this product."
- "Argue against buying this. What would make me regret it in a year?"

Then do the two things the AI can't do for you. Open the seller's page and check the price, model number, delivery cost and return terms. And look at one source the chatbot didn't pick, such as a consumer magazine's test.
