You can now type "a sad country song about a broken tractor" into an app and get back a finished track, with verses, a chorus and a convincing human-sounding singer, in under a minute. That has set off three fights at once: record labels against the companies that make these tools, streaming services against a flood of machine-made uploads, and singers against copies of their own voice.

## How does an AI song generator work?

Music generators are a kind of [generative AI](/glossary/generative-ai), the same family of technology as chatbots and image makers. The company feeds a [model](/glossary/model) a very large collection of recorded songs, and the model learns the patterns: how a chorus usually builds, what a guitar sounds like under a voice, how a word gets stretched over a note. When you type a prompt, it produces new audio that follows those patterns.

The model doesn't store a library of songs and stitch them together. But it can only learn from what it was given, so the [training data](/glossary/training-data) is where the legal arguments start. If a model learned from millions of copyrighted recordings, did it need permission? And if it can produce something very close to a famous song, is that a copy? Our explainer on [how AI models are trained](/understand/how-ai-models-are-trained) covers the general process.

## Who are the big services, and why were they sued?

The two best-known song generators are Suno and Udio. In June 2024 the US record industry body, the RIAA, sued both on behalf of the three major labels, Universal Music Group, Sony Music and Warner Music Group, alleging "mass infringement" of copyright, as [Music Business Worldwide reported](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/universal-music-settles-udio-lawsuit-strikes-deal-for-licensed-ai-music-platform). The companies argue that training on music is fair use, a US legal defence that allows some uses of copyrighted work without permission.

Since late 2025, most of those fights have turned into business deals.

- **Universal and Udio** settled on 29 October 2025. They agreed a new subscription service, due in 2026, where users can make, stream and share music in what they call a "licensed and protected environment". Until then, Udio said creations would stay in a "walled garden", with "fingerprinting, filtering, and other measures" added.
- **Warner and Udio** settled on 19 November 2025, according to [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/warner-music-settles-udio-lawsuit-strikes-licensing-deal-with-ai-platform). The planned service will use the voices and songs of artists and songwriters "who choose to participate".
- **Warner and Suno** settled on 25 November 2025, [TheWrap reported](https://www.thewrap.com/warner-music-group-partnership-suno-settles-lawsuit-ai-music-company). According to [Billboard](https://billboard.com/pro/suno-warner-music-sign-ai-licensing-deal-settle-lawsuit/), songs made on Suno's free tier can no longer be downloaded, paid users face monthly download limits, and Warner artists can choose whether their name, likeness, voice and songs are used.

In September 2026 Suno released new models, called v6, which it says were trained from scratch on music licensed from partners including Warner, BMG and Believe. Universal and Sony catalogues are not included, [Music Business Worldwide reports](https://musicbusinessworldwide.com/suno-v6-ai-music-models-launch-in-partnership-with-wmg-bmg-and-believe). We could not confirm whether the new Udio service has launched.

## Which lawsuits are still going?

Two majors are still fighting.

**Universal and Sony against Suno.** The original 2024 case continues in a US court in Boston. In August 2026 the labels added a claim that Suno got its training audio by getting around YouTube's anti-download protections. In its reply, Suno admitted it "obtained audio data from YouTube for use as training data" but argued that training is "quintessential fair use", [CMU reports](https://completemusicupdate.com/suno-unveils-upgrade-developed-with-music-industry-partners-while-continuing-to-battle-the-music-industry-in-court/).

**Sony against Udio.** Sony is now the only major label without a Udio licence. On 20 July 2026 it filed a second lawsuit in New York, alleging Udio copied 30,117 recordings to train its models, after a judge refused to add those songs to the first case. Udio has argued that its use of copyrighted music is fair use, according to [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/sony-music-files-new-lawsuit-against-ai-platform-udio-asserting-over-30000-sound-recordings-a-judge-barred-it-from-adding-to-its-original-case/).

Europe has produced the first big ruling against a song generator. On 31 July 2026, according to a [statement from GEMA](https://www.gema.de/en/w/suno-entscheidung-2026), Germany's music rights society, a Munich court ruled that Suno had broken copyright by training on GEMA's repertoire and must pay licence fees. GEMA says it showed that Suno could output songs closely matching hits such as "Forever Young", "Mambo No. 5" and "Daddy Cool". That is GEMA's account; it does not say whether Suno will appeal. A related Munich ruling about song lyrics and ChatGPT is covered in our guide to [who owns AI-generated content](/live-with-it/who-owns-ai-generated-content).

## How much AI music is on streaming services?

A lot is uploaded, and very little is listened to. Deezer, the French streaming service, runs its own detection tool and publishes the numbers.

- In January 2025 about 10,000 fully AI-made tracks arrived on Deezer each day, 10% of uploads, [TechCrunch reports](https://techcrunch.com/2026/07/21/music-streamer-deezer-says-more-than-50-of-daily-uploads-are-ai-generated/).
- By April 2026 it was about 75,000 a day, or 44%. In July 2026 Deezer said AI tracks had passed 50% of daily uploads.
- Yet AI tracks accounted for only 1 to 3% of streams in April, and Deezer flagged 85% of the streams on those tracks as fraudulent, [according to its figures](https://alternativeto.net/news/2026/4/deezer-says-44-of-daily-uploads-are-ai-generated-but-only-represent-1-3-of-streams/).

That last number explains much of the flood. Streaming fraud means uploading cheap tracks and playing them with bots to collect royalties, which come out of the same pot that pays human artists.

## How do platforms label or limit AI songs?

They take very different approaches.

**Deezer** labels the AI-generated tracks it detects, removes them from its algorithmic recommendations and editorial playlists, and does not pay for streams it flags as fraudulent. In 2026 it also began deleting AI tracks that had not been played in six months or were linked to fraud.

**Spotify** does not label AI music on its own. Instead, since April 2026 it shows "AI credits" in a test version of its mobile app, under Song credits and in the Now Playing view. They say which part of a song used AI, such as lyrics, vocals, instruments or production. Artists add them through their distributor, and it is voluntary: Spotify itself says "the absence of AI credits doesn't mean AI wasn't used on a song", [according to Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/spotify-to-show-ai-tags-in-song-credits-where-artists-have-chosen-to-disclose-through-their-label-or-distributor/). Spotify also says it removed more than 75 million "spammy tracks" in the year to September 2025 and filters mass uploads, duplicates and very short tracks out of its recommendations.

**Bandcamp** went furthest. In January 2026 it banned music made "wholly or in substantial part by AI", and banned using AI to impersonate other artists, [TechCrunch reported](https://techcrunch.com/2026/01/14/bandcamp-takes-a-stand-against-ai-music-banning-it-from-the-platform/).

## Can someone copy a singer's voice?

Yes, and fairly easily. The case that made this famous was "Heart on My Sleeve" in 2023, a track by an anonymous creator called ghostwriter that used AI copies of the voices of Drake and The Weeknd. It spread widely before Universal had it taken down from Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube and others, [NBC News reported](https://www.nbcnews.com/pop-culture/viral-ai-powered-drake-weeknd-song-removed-streaming-services-rcna80098).

Platform rules now mostly forbid it. Spotify's policy, announced in September 2025, says "vocal impersonation is only allowed in music on Spotify when the impersonated artist has authorized the usage". The licensed services that Suno and Udio are building with labels rest on the same idea: artists opt in and get paid.

The law is catching up unevenly.

- **Tennessee** passed the ELVIS Act in March 2024, adding "voice" to its existing protection of a person's name and likeness, [the governor's office said](https://www.tn.gov/governor/news/2024/3/21/photos--gov--lee-signs-elvis-act-into-law.html).
- **US federal law.** The NO FAKES Act would give every person, not just celebrities, a right over AI copies of their voice and likeness, with a takedown process for platforms. A Senate committee approved it in June 2026, according to [Holland & Knight](https://hklaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/06/senate-judiciary-committee-advances-legislation-to-protect-name). It still needs votes in the full Senate and the House.
- **Denmark** introduced a bill in October 2026 to ban sharing realistic deepfakes of people's faces and voices without consent, including imitations of artists' performances, [Euronews reports](https://www.euronews.com/2026/10/08/denmark-introduces-a-bill-protecting-peoples-faces-and-voices-from-deepfakes). The culture minister hopes it takes effect in early 2027.

Copied voices are also used to cheat ordinary people out of money, which is a separate problem: see our guide to [AI voice clone scams](/live-with-it/ai-voice-clone-scams).

## What do EU rules say?

Two parts of the [EU AI Act](/glossary/ai-act) matter for music.

**Training.** Since 2 August 2025, companies that make large general-purpose AI models must have a copyright policy, respect creators who have opted out of text and data mining, and publish a summary of what they trained on, according to [Clifford Chance](https://www.cliffordchance.com/insights/resources/blogs/ip-insights/2025/10/copyright-compliance-under-the-eu-ai-act-for-gpai-model-providers.html). The EU's AI Office gained the power to fine them from 2 August 2026. Whether dedicated song generators count as general-purpose models is a question for lawyers; the German GEMA case was decided under ordinary copyright law.

**Labelling.** Under [Article 50](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/), tools that generate synthetic audio must mark it in a machine-readable way so it can be detected as AI-made. Anyone who publishes a [deepfake](/glossary/deepfake), such as a fake recording of a real singer, must say it is artificial. For obviously artistic or satirical work, that disclosure can be done in a way that "does not hamper the display or enjoyment of the work". These rules apply from 2 August 2026, with tools already on the market given until 2 December 2026 to add the marking, [Goodwin writes](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/publications/2026/08/alerts-technology-dpc-eu-ai-act-transparency-obligations-now-in-force). Our [EU AI Act explainer](/live-with-it/eu-ai-act-explained) covers the rest of the law.

Whether you own a song you made with AI is a separate question again, answered in [who owns AI-generated content](/live-with-it/who-owns-ai-generated-content).

## What can listeners do?

- **Check the credits.** On Spotify's mobile app, open Song credits; on Deezer, look for its AI label. Remember that no label doesn't prove a human made it.
- **Be wary of unknown "artists" with huge catalogues** and no live shows, interviews or social media history. That pattern often goes with bulk AI uploads.
- **Report impersonation.** If a track pretends to be by an artist you know, report it in the app. Spotify says it is speeding up its review of music delivered to the wrong artist's profile.
- **Pay artists directly** if you want your money to reach human musicians, for example by buying music at concerts or from their own shops.

Our guide to [spotting AI-generated content](/live-with-it/spotting-ai-generated-content) has more general tips.

## What can musicians do?

- **Watch your artist profile.** Check regularly for releases you didn't make, and report them through your distributor and the platform.
- **Reserve your rights in writing.** In the EU, AI developers must respect opt-outs from text and data mining. Ask your label, publisher or collecting society (such as GEMA) whether they have registered one for your work.
- **Read the opt-in terms.** Several label deals say artists can choose whether their voice and songs are used for AI. Ask your label what you have agreed to and how you are paid.
- **Disclose your own AI use** honestly if your distributor supports Spotify-style AI credits. Spotify says disclosure does not lower a track's ranking.
